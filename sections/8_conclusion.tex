\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This thesis asked how an endpoint on a path-aware Internet should choose the best use of its paths given
the intention of the application and the state of the network. It answered in two movements that share one
idea, drawn from Mortise~\cite{Shen2026}: treat application intent as a first-class input that reshapes a
network mechanism, not as a constant compiled into it.

\Cref{ch:single_path} turned a fixed-objective DQN path selector for SCION~\cite{Bourak2025} into an
intent-conditioned one. Two design decisions carried the work: a permutation-equivariant \emph{scoring}
architecture that handles a variable, changing set of candidate paths, and the routing of the intent vector
into the \emph{per-path comparison} itself, which lets a single policy serve a range of objectives --
including ones it never trained on -- without retraining. \Cref{ch:multipath} asked what happens if we use several paths at once and optimize the
application itself, and built a
two-timescale hierarchical agent pair that co-optimizes video bitrate and per-path traffic split over
Multipath QUIC, coupled so that each agent accounts for the other -- then conditioned that pair on the
intent as well, so that one policy serves the range of application objectives three separately trained
ones had covered.

\paragraph{What we learned.} The best-supported finding is the one the second system was built to test:
\emph{the two decisions are not separable}. Rate and split are coupled through the same bottleneck, and
a controller that fixes either is optimizing against a constraint it could have moved -- removing
either agent forfeits most of the achievable quality, and the joint pair leads every comparison point
under every application intent at margins several times the spread between training runs of the same
method --- on the analytical backend under all three intents, and on the packet-level backend too once
the policy is trained there rather than transferred to it. Close behind it is an architectural lesson we had expected to be a convenience and found to be
load-bearing. Scoring each candidate with shared weights is normally adopted so that a policy can
accommodate a variable action set; substituting a conventional fixed-dimension head costs more quality
than any other single change we measured, and enough that the system's headline claim reverses
outright. It is also the smaller network of the two, on both problems, and its size does not move with
the path count while the head it replaces cannot be loaded at a path count it was not trained on.
Whatever a dynamic-input design costs, on these problems it is not parameters.

Second, that the payoff of joint rate-and-split control is \emph{not} a fixed quantity, and that the
variable governing it is not the one we expected. It is tempting to say such control matters when the
network is volatile; what we measured is that it matters when path capacity is unevenly distributed
relative to what an even split would place on each path, and that churn and bursts matter because they
destroy that evenness rather than because motion is itself the relevant property. A stationary but
asymmetric network defeats application-only adaptation as thoroughly as a churning one, and so does
dividing a fixed capacity among more paths. The payoff also
scales with what the application asks for: the stricter its delay budget, the more a poor allocation
costs. Mapping the two axes together taught us that they are not separable --- the intents differ
fourteen times more from one another on a harsh network than on a benign one --- and, less comfortably,
that they bound our own headline result. The learned pair beats every hand-written policy on the network
it trained on and on a milder one it had never seen, and loses to a plain congestion-control heuristic
on a harsher one, with its training-seed spread rising by an order of magnitude as it goes. Where a
learned scheduler is measured decides not only how large its advantage looks but whether it has one.

Third, the thread that joins the two systems, stated with the boundary we found on it. \emph{Where}
intent is injected matters more than \emph{that} it is injected: for a comparison-based selector, a
conditioning signal reaching only the terms shared by every candidate cannot change the decision,
because it cancels under the comparison -- exactly and on the whole action when that comparison is a
softmax. The intent has to reach each alternative's own features to reorder them. But the guarantee
turned out to be narrower than the behavior, and this is the correction we did not anticipate. The
cancellation assumes the conditioning is additive on the terms the comparison consumes, and three
mechanisms in an ordinary architecture break that assumption, so a trained nonlinear encoder routes
intent past a cancellation the theory says should stop it. The principle survives as a statement about
which routings carry a guarantee rather than which happen to work, which is the form a designer can
actually use. One of the three mechanisms taught us something we had not gone looking for: a normalizer
derived from the objective is a conditioning path nobody declared, and it cost real quality until we
closed it, at which point the two routings we had been comparing turned out to be equally good. Audit
the preprocessing for the signal you were careful about routing. Both settings also taught us how much
such conditioning can be worth at all: it is cheap
and it costs nothing to add, but it earns something only where the objectives genuinely conflict and
there is headroom to exploit, and neither of our environments offered much of either.

A fourth lesson is about method rather than mechanism, and we learned it the expensive way, in both
systems. Ten results in this thesis looked clean at one sampling of the training seed and did not survive
a larger one. Five are in \cref{ch:multipath} --- four reported from a single training run and retracted
at three, one surviving three and six seeds on its pooled statistic and failing when that statistic was
decomposed per seed --- and five more in \cref{ch:single_path}, found by applying the same rule to the
chapter that had motivated it (\cref{sec:p1eval:limits}). The single-path five are milder: no mechanism
broke, and what fell was the exactness of three claims and the third decimal of two others read off a
favorable run. That the same failure appears in both systems, at different severities, is what makes it a
property of the method rather than of one experiment. The cause is the same in both:
on this problem the spread between training runs exceeds the confidence interval over evaluation
episodes by an order of magnitude. Sampling training seeds is necessary and not sufficient: a cheap
simulator can be faithful enough to train usable policies in while being uncorrelated with the realistic
body on which of them is good --- and, once we measured it, expensive in the only currency that matters,
since training on the realistic body instead beat transferring to it by 0.228 QoE. Nor is it sufficient
to sample seeds and then pool over them: a summary
statistic computed across seeds before its sign is checked within them can turn ``four runs agree and
two disagree'' into one confident-looking number. We report the retractions as a result because the
pattern is more useful than any of the numbers it corrected, and because it is the one finding here that
generalizes beyond multipath scheduling.

\paragraph{Future work.} Several threads remain open, and much of the draft's structure is deliberately laid
out to guide them. On the experimental side, the question \cref{sec:p2eval:ns3} raised --- whether the system \emph{learns}
on the packet-level body rather than merely transferring to it --- is now answered at three seeds and in
the affirmative (\cref{sec:p2eval:ns3native}), which relocates rather than closes it: that arm is
unconditioned and covers one scenario, so the natural next run is a packet-level-native
\emph{conditioned} policy, which would put the chapter's conditioning results on the same footing as its
headline one. Alongside it,
testing both systems on topologies larger than those they trained on ---
the path-count axis of that question is now measured and the topology-size axis is not. On
the system side: replacing the TCP-like subflows with a real Multipath QUIC transport, and enriching the
VMAF surrogate so quality responds to delivery conditions and not just bitrate -- a change that would also
close the queueing-fidelity gap that makes the analytical backend blind to the variable deciding
packet-level outcomes. On the research side, three directions stand out: \emph{inferring} intent from
traffic rather than assuming it is declared; \emph{online and safe} learning against live traffic with
bounded worst-case behavior; and unifying the two conditioned controllers, which this thesis builds and
evaluates separately, into a single cross-layer policy in which one declared intent steers path
selection, traffic split, and application output together.
