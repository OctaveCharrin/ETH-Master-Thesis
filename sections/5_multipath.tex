\chapter{Joint Application--Network Optimization over Multipath}
\label{ch:multipath}

\Cref{ch:single_path} chose a single path for a flow and found its ceiling: when the shared bottleneck
saturates, even the best single path cannot carry the demand. This chapter starts from a more ambitious
premise. SCION offers not one path but several, so why commit to one? And if Mortise~\cite{Shen2026} can
tune a \emph{transport mechanism} to the application, why not tune the \emph{application itself} in the
same breath? We design and evaluate a system that (i) uses multiple paths at once via Multipath
QUIC~\cite{DeConinck2017} and (ii) jointly optimizes what the application sends -- the video encoder's
target bitrate -- and how the network spreads it -- the per-path traffic split -- to maximize the quality
of experience of a real-time video call. \Cref{sec:mp:design} presents the design, \cref{sec:mp:impl} the
testbed, and \cref{sec:mp:eval} the evaluation.

\paragraph{What this chapter assumes of SCION, and what it does not.} The substrate is worth declaring
before anything is built on it. This chapter uses exactly one property of a path-aware architecture:
that an endpoint is given \emph{several} usable end-to-end paths at once and may send on all of them.
It does not run a SCION control plane. There is no beaconing, no ISD structure, no path store, and no
segment composition; the six paths are a hand-specified configuration of per-path bottleneck rates and
delays (\cref{tab:app:p2topo}), and path ``churn'' is a hazard process rather than a control-plane
event. That is the ordinary abstraction for a transport study, because the question here is scheduling
and rate control rather than path discovery, and it has two consequences worth naming. The results are
portable to any multipath substrate, which is a strength. And the natural union of this thesis's two
halves --- a learned selector choosing which paths to place under a learned scheduler --- is
consequently untested, which is the honest form of the future-work item in \cref{ch:conclusion}.

Three findings organize the chapter. Neither decision can be made well alone: removing the transport
agent forfeits most of the achievable quality and removing the application agent forfeits a fifth of
it.

\section{System Design: Co-Optimizing Rate and Traffic Split}
\label{sec:mp:design}

\Cref{sec:p2design:motivation} motivates the joint, multipath framing and the strawmen it improves on.
\Cref{sec:p2design:hierarchy} presents the two-timescale hierarchical agent pair.
\Cref{sec:p2design:mdp} specifies the observations, actions, and rewards.
\Cref{sec:p2design:coupling} explains the coupling that keeps the two agents cooperating rather than
working at cross purposes. \Cref{sec:p2design:scoring} extends the scoring idea of \cref{ch:single_path}
from a discrete choice to a continuous split over a variable, possibly changing, path set.

\subsection{Motivation and Strawmen}
\label{sec:p2design:motivation}

Interactive video is a demanding, ubiquitous workload with no playback buffer to hide variance: a frame
that misses its deadline is simply lost. Its QoE depends on two decisions that are usually made in
isolation. The \emph{application} chooses an encoding bitrate -- too high and the network drops frames,
too low and quality suffers. The \emph{network} chooses how to carry the resulting bytes -- over one path
or spread across several. Optimizing either alone is a strawman:

\begin{description}
  \item[Application-only.] Adapt bitrate to the aggregate network but carry it over a fixed or naive
  path assignment. This wastes SCION's path diversity and, as our results show, collapses when the paths
  it depends on degrade.
  \item[Network-only.] Schedule cleverly across paths but treat the offered load as exogenous. The
  scheduler cannot relieve congestion it is itself creating by admitting too high a bitrate.
\end{description}

The two decisions are coupled through the same bottleneck: bitrate sets the load the scheduler must
place, and the scheduler's success sets the effective capacity the bitrate controller sees. Optimizing
them jointly is the design's premise. The challenge is that they naturally operate on \emph{different
timescales} -- an encoder should not change bitrate every frame, but a scheduler must react per frame --
which motivates a hierarchy rather than a single monolithic agent.

\subsection{Two-Timescale Hierarchical Agents}
\label{sec:p2design:hierarchy}

We split control into two cooperating agents that act at different cadences, a ``brain and body''
decomposition.

\begin{description}
  \item[Application agent (slow).] Every \SI{1}{\second} (once per 30 frames at 30\,fps) it observes
  an aggregate summary of the connection and outputs a single target encoder bitrate in the range
  \SIrange{300}{6000}{\kilo\bit\per\second}. It is deliberately \emph{horizon-agnostic} -- it never
  observes how far along the call is -- so the same policy applies to a call of any length, an essential
  property for a controller meant to run in unbounded real sessions.
  \item[Transport agent (fast).] Every frame (${\approx}\SI{33}{\milli\second}$) it observes per-path
  transport state \emph{and the application agent's current target bitrate}, and outputs a split of that
  frame's bytes across the available paths.
\end{description}

Both are trained with soft actor-critic (\cref{sec:background:rl}), whose continuous action support and
sample efficiency suit these controls and the cost of simulator rollouts.

observes, the bitrate signal that makes the pair hierarchical rather than merely concurrent, and the
two reward signals. The sections that follow specify each of its parts.

\subsection{Observations, Actions, and Rewards}
\label{sec:p2design:mdp}

\paragraph{Application observation and action.} The application agent sees a small fixed-size vector:
normalized current bitrate, smoothed RTT, jitter, loss, and throughput. Its action is one continuous
scalar in $[-1,1]$, mapped affinely to a target bitrate in \SIrange{300}{6000}{\kilo\bit\per\second}.

\paragraph{Transport observation and action.} The transport agent sees a global block (target bitrate,
RTT, loss, throughput) and, for each path, a feature vector (congestion window, smoothed RTT, buffer
occupancy, per-path throughput, per-path loss). Its action is an $N$-vector passed through a softmax to
yield a split $(w_1,\dots,w_N)$ that is non-negative and sums to one -- a valid allocation by
construction.

\paragraph{Rewards.} The rewards encode the QoE objective and the division of responsibility between the
agents. The application's per-window QoE reward composes a quality term with penalties:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{QoE} = a\cdot\frac{\mathrm{VMAF}(\cdot)}{100}
             - b\cdot\widetilde{\mathrm{lat}}
             - c\cdot\widetilde{\mathrm{jit}}
             - d\cdot\mathrm{loss}
             + e\cdot(1-\mathrm{loss})\cdot\widetilde{\mathrm{util}},
\qquad \text{clipped to } [-2, 1],
\label{eq:p2qoe}
\end{equation}
where $\widetilde{\mathrm{lat}}$ and $\widetilde{\mathrm{jit}}$ are latency and jitter normalized by
$\mathit{lat}_{\mathrm{norm}}$ and $\mathit{jit}_{\mathrm{norm}}$ and clipped, and
$\widetilde{\mathrm{util}}$ is the achieved bitrate normalized by
\SI{3300}{\kilo\bit\per\second} -- the knee of the quality curve. The utilization term restores the
rate-control gradient under a quality model that saturates in bitrate; it carries $e = 0.25$ in the
dynamic scenario and is harmless under the default logarithmic curve. The quality term uses a VMAF
surrogate (\cref{sec:mp:impl}); when a learned surrogate that already accounts for loss is used, the
explicit loss penalty is dropped to avoid double-counting.

The weight vector $(a,b,c,d,e)$ together with the frame deadline is not a constant of the system but
the \emph{application intent} this thesis is about, and the three profiles evaluated in
\cref{sec:mp:eval} instantiate it at three points of the ITU-T G.1010 use-case space
(\cref{tab:p2profiles}). The transport agent receives a per-frame reward that credits delivery without
quality, since it does not control bitrate:
\begin{equation}
R_{\text{transport}} = (1 - \mathrm{loss}) - b\cdot\widetilde{\mathrm{lat}} - c\cdot\widetilde{\mathrm{jit}},
\qquad \text{clipped to } [-2, 1].
\label{eq:p2transport}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Where these weights come from.} A reward whose weights are chosen by hand invites the
objection that the system was tuned into its own result, so we derive them instead. The delay anchors
are ITU-T G.114's~\cite{ITUG114} --- \SI{150}{\milli\second} preferred and \SI{400}{\milli\second}
unacceptable for interactive use --- and the jitter normalizer is Y.1541's~\cite{ITUY1541} class-0 IP
delay-variation objective. The delay and loss weights $(b, d)$ are then \emph{fit} rather than set: we
grid-search them for the pair that maximizes the rank correlation between the linear reward of
\cref{eq:p2qoe} and a G.1070~\cite{ITUG1070} opinion-model reference evaluated over a corpus of
delivery traces. Two properties of that fit matter. It is corpus-insensitive: a synthetic corpus and
one drawn from recorded traces both land on $b = c = 0.25$ and $d = 2.0$. And it is gated on
\emph{stability} as well as correlation --- a weight set that changes which policy is ranked best is
rejected however well it correlates, because a reward whose optimum moves with its own free parameters
cannot grade a scheduler. That gate is a statement about when a reward may be used to rank policies at
all, and it anticipates the reading \cref{sec:p2eval:regimes} gives the static scenario, where the
ranking again turns out not to be stable --- there against the spread between training runs rather than
against the reward's parameters.

\subsection{Coupling the Two Agents}
\label{sec:p2design:coupling}

The design's subtlety is keeping the agents cooperative. Two mechanisms couple them.

\paragraph{Observation coupling.} The transport agent observes the application's current target bitrate.
It therefore schedules \emph{knowing} how much load it must place, and can, for instance, spread an
aggressive bitrate across more paths rather than overrunning one. This is what makes the pair hierarchical
rather than two agents optimizing in ignorance of each other.

\paragraph{Reward decomposition.} The application owns quality (the VMAF term) and is credited for the
latency, jitter, and loss experienced over the window its bitrate governed; the transport agent is
credited only for how well it delivered each frame. This assigns each decision the consequences it can
actually control -- the encoder is not blamed for a poor split, nor the scheduler for an over-ambitious
bitrate -- while the shared bottleneck still couples them: a bad split shows up as latency/loss in the
application's reward, and an over-high bitrate shows up as loss the transport agent cannot schedule away.

\subsection{Continuous Scoring over a Variable Path Set}
\label{sec:p2design:scoring}

The transport agent inherits the single-path selector's problem -- a variable, possibly changing number
of paths -- and its solution, adapted from a discrete choice to a continuous allocation. A shared per-path
encoder maps each $(\text{global}, \text{path}_i)$ pair to a logit; the logits are softmaxed into the
split, so the policy is permutation-equivariant and independent of the path count. To handle paths that
appear and disappear, each path carries a \emph{liveness} flag and the state includes a binary mask; the
softmax excludes dead paths, so traffic is never allocated to a path that is gone. The critic aggregates
per-path embeddings with a masked mean-pool -- a Deep Sets~\cite{Zaheer2017} operation -- yielding a value
estimate invariant to path ordering and count. This continuous scoring agent is the natural sibling of the
discrete scoring selector of \cref{ch:single_path}: the same architectural idea, once for ``pick a path''
and once for ``divide across paths.''

\subsection{Where Intent Must Enter a Continuous Allocation}
\label{sec:p2design:cancel}

\Cref{prop:p1cancel} showed that conditioning entering only path-independent terms cancels under the
$\arg\max$ and cannot change a discrete selection. The allocation here is not an $\arg\max$ but a
softmax, so the question must be asked again.

We state at the outset what the answer is and what weight it should carry, because the material that
follows is easy to over-read. The result is a \emph{design lemma}, not a theorem: it is the observation
that a softmax is invariant to a constant added to every logit --- an identity so standard that our own
implementation already relies on it for numerical stability. Weighed as a theoretical contribution it
would not survive, since its premise is violated three ways by the architecture this chapter deploys and
\cref{sec:p2eval:conditioning} shows its behavioral prediction to be false on trained policies. Weighed
as what it is --- a statement of which conditioning routings carry a \emph{guarantee}, together with a
measurement of exactly how ordinary architectures escape that guarantee --- it is the more useful of the
two, and it is the reason this section is here. The lemma first, then the three mechanisms that break
it.

\begin{proposition}[Cancellation under a softmax allocation]
\label{prop:p2cancel}
Let a transport policy allocate traffic by $\mathbf{p} = \operatorname{softmax}(z_1,\dots,z_N)$ with
logits $z_i = f(s, f_i) + c(s, \mathbf{w})$, where $f_i$ is path~$i$'s feature row and the intent
$\mathbf{w}$ enters only through the term $c$, which does not depend on~$i$. Then $\mathbf{p}$ is
independent of $\mathbf{w}$: the policy consumes the intent and emits an identical split.
\end{proposition}

\noindent The proof is one line. Writing $z_i = \hat{z}_i + c$ with $\hat{z}_i = f(s,f_i)$,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{softmax}(\hat{\mathbf{z}} + c\mathbf{1})_i
  = \frac{e^{\hat{z}_i + c}}{\sum_j e^{\hat{z}_j + c}}
  = \frac{e^{c}\,e^{\hat{z}_i}}{e^{c}\sum_j e^{\hat{z}_j}}
  = \operatorname{softmax}(\hat{\mathbf{z}})_i ,
\label{eq:p2softmaxshift}
\end{equation}
so the intent-dependent factor cancels between numerator and denominator. The implementation already
relies on this identity for a different reason: \texttt{PathAgent.to\_split} subtracts
$\max_i z_i$ before exponentiating for numerical stability, which is only sound because the subtraction
provably does not change the output.

Two things make this statement stronger than its discrete counterpart. It is an equality on the
\emph{whole action}, not merely on which index wins: the split is numerically identical in every
component, so there is no tie-breaking caveat of the kind that qualifies \cref{prop:p1cancel}. And it is
checkable to machine precision rather than behaviorally --- hold the path rows fixed, sweep the intent
through the global block alone, and the resulting splits must agree to floating-point tolerance. We
measure exactly this, and the measurement is where the interesting part begins.

\paragraph{The premise is narrow, and the deployed architecture violates it three times over.}
\Cref{prop:p2cancel} requires the intent to be additive \emph{on the logits the softmax consumes} ---
which is a materially stronger requirement than ``the intent enters through a path-independent block''.
Note first what it does \emph{not} require: separability is a statement about the decomposition, not
about linearity, and both $f$ and $c$ may be arbitrary nonlinear networks. \Cref{tab:p2cancel} reports
the behavioral divergence of the split as the intent is swept, for a ladder of scorers running from the
proposition's exact hypothesis up to the policy this chapter actually deploys.

The first row is the proposition, confirmed to $3.8\times10^{-9}$ --- float32 noise. The next three rows
each remove one of its assumptions, and each is a mechanism worth naming, because together they say
something the proposition alone does not.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The squash.} The per-path latent is $\tanh$-squashed before the softmax, as the Gaussian
  policy's bounded action mapping requires, and $\tanh(z + c) - \tanh(z)$ depends on $z$. A constant
  added upstream of a saturating nonlinearity is no longer a constant downstream of it. This is the
  sharpest of the three and it is architectural: \emph{no} $\tanh$-Gaussian policy satisfies
  \cref{prop:p2cancel}'s premise, whatever it does with its conditioning.
  \item \textbf{The shared encoder is not separable.} The deployed scorer is an MLP over the
  \emph{concatenation} $[\,s \,\|\, f_i\,]$, so the global block mixes with each path's own features
  inside the hidden layers. Its first layer \emph{is} affine on that concatenation, so the intent enters
  the first pre-activation exactly common-mode --- verified to $1.2\times10^{-7}$ --- and it is the first
  ReLU that destroys the invariance. The proposition therefore describes the architecture for exactly
  one layer.
  \item \textbf{The observation normalizer carries the intent.} Each path's smoothed RTT is divided by a
  latency normalizer that is itself part of the intent bundle (\SI{200}{\milli\second} for
  \texttt{interactive}, \SI{800}{\milli\second} for \texttt{passive}), so switching intent rescales a
  per-path feature column by $4\times$ even when the conditioning is nominally global-only: the measured
  per-path row span is 0.300 rather than 0. This is precisely the trust-feature leak that qualifies
  \cref{prop:p1cancel} in \cref{sec:p1design:concat}, reappearing through a normalizer instead of a
  feature. Fixing the normalizers across intents closes it exactly (row span 0.000, last row).
\end{enumerate}

What survives is both weaker and more useful than what we set out to prove. The exact claim is
\cref{prop:p2cancel} and it holds for additively separable scorers; it discharges, for that function
class, the assertion in \cref{sec:rel:gcrl} that the cancellation covers ``argmax \emph{or} softmax''
selectors. The design principle it licenses is the portable one and it is unchanged: \emph{any}
comparison-based selector must route conditioning into its per-alternative terms if the conditioning is
to be guaranteed to do anything. But the corrected version says more than the original would have. A
global slot is not reliably \emph{inert} either --- three independent mechanisms leak intent past it ---
so per-path injection is not merely the better of two options but the only construction with a guarantee
in either direction. \Cref{sec:p2eval:conditioning} measures what that distinction is worth on trained
policies, and the answer is not the one this section predicts.

\section{Implementation: Multipath Real-Time Video Testbed}
\label{sec:mp:impl}

Realizing the design requires a testbed that is faithful enough to expose real multipath and
congestion behavior yet fast enough for reinforcement-learning rollouts. We resolve this tension with a
single data-plane abstraction served by two interchangeable backends (\cref{sec:p2impl:dataplane}): a
packet-level NS-3 simulator and a fast analytical mock. \Cref{sec:p2impl:rt} describes the real-time
video and multipath model, \cref{sec:p2impl:agents} the agent implementation and the VMAF surrogate, and
\cref{sec:p2impl:gaps} the known gaps that scope the current results.

\subsection{Data-Plane Abstraction and Two Backends}
\label{sec:p2impl:dataplane}

Both backends implement one abstract \texttt{DataPlane} interface, so the RL code, observations, and
rewards are written once and run unchanged against either. This lets us iterate quickly on the mock and
validate on the higher-fidelity simulator without divergence.

\begin{description}
  \item[NS-3 backend.] A packet-level simulation in which the connection's several paths are modeled as
  independent subflows over a topology of links, each with its own capacity, propagation delay, and
  active-queue management (FqCoDel). Realistic contention comes from OnOff UDP cross-traffic with
  exponentially distributed on/off periods, phase-shifted per path so the paths do not degrade in
  lockstep. Each video frame's bytes are striped across the subflows according to the transport agent's
  split, and delivery is tracked by a byte-watermark scheme -- avoiding per-packet tagging -- from which
  per-frame latency, jitter, and deadline-miss loss are measured. Python and NS-3 communicate through an
  ns3-ai shared-memory bridge.
  \item[Mock backend.] A pure-Python, per-seed-deterministic model in which each path has a capacity
  envelope (a smooth sinusoidal component plus bursty cross-traffic and noise) and a standing-queue model
  that produces bufferbloat when a path is overdriven. It runs orders of magnitude faster than NS-3 and is
  used for rapid training.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Non-stationary dynamics.} Both backends inject four mechanisms that repeatedly unbalance the
capacity available on each path: \emph{path churn} (paths appear and disappear, and bytes sent to a dead
path are lost), \emph{regime shifts} (abrupt changes in the best path's capacity), \emph{congestion bursts}
(transient per-path capacity collapses), and \emph{correlated failures} (shared-bottleneck groups that
degrade together). The NS-3 body implements churn with drop-all receive error models rather than by
collapsing the link rate, which would strand a packet mid-serialization, and tears down and reconnects
the affected subflow; regime, burst, and correlation scale the bottleneck rate and the cross-traffic
rate together. Behavioral parity between the two bodies is checked by a fixed-policy loss-regime guard
(\texttt{rl-mpquic/scripts/parity\_check.py}).

\paragraph{Parity between the bodies, and what it does and does not say.} Driving both backends with the
same neutral policy --- an even split at a fixed \SI{1500}{\kilo\bit\per\second}, no agents --- puts them
in the same loss regime: mean per-frame loss is 0.148 on the mock against 0.190 on NS-3 with TCP
subflows and 0.200 with UDP, and no configuration expires more than 1.6\% of frames. Parity is
\emph{behavioral} rather than frame-identical by design, because the two bodies draw from independent
random streams, so the guard is a band (a gap under 0.15) and not an equality. The bodies are also
deliberately not identical in one way that matters: the mock's sender queue is unbounded where NS-3's is
not, which is why the mock's failure model is optimistic and is the mechanism behind the \texttt{single}
baseline inverting between them (\cref{tab:p2ns3}).

Decomposing by mechanism sharpens what ``four non-stationary mechanisms'' should be taken to mean. Run
one at a time on NS-3, \emph{churn alone} produces essentially all of the loss (0.219), while regime
shifts alone and congestion bursts alone each produce \textbf{0.000}. They reduce capacity, but at this
offered rate not below what an even split demands, so they bite only in combination or under a policy
driving a higher bitrate. The scenario's difficulty under a neutral policy is therefore a churn
phenomenon; the other three mechanisms shape what a \emph{rate-adaptive} controller experiences rather
than what a fixed one does.

\subsection{Real-Time Video and Multipath Model}
\label{sec:p2impl:rt}

The video source mirrors a conferencing encoder: a fixed frame rate (30\,fps by default), variable
frame sizes (with periodic larger I-frames and per-frame jitter), no playback buffer, and a deadline
after which a late frame is discarded as lost. The aggregate target bitrate ranges over
\SIrange{300}{6000}{\kilo\bit\per\second}. The canonical topology is deliberately \emph{non-dominant}: no
single path can carry full-quality video, and the aggregate usable capacity sits below the quality knee,
so the system must genuinely combine paths and trade rate against delivery. For example, a four-path
configuration mixes paths of differing capacity and RTT -- including a higher-capacity but high-latency
``trap'' path -- under heavy, path-specific cross-traffic, so that neither the widest nor the
lowest-latency path is right on its own.

\subsection{Agents and QoE Surrogate}
\label{sec:p2impl:agents}

\paragraph{Agents.} Both agents are soft actor-critic learners with twin critics and automatic
temperature tuning. The transport agent has two variants: a \emph{flat} version whose actor and critic
consume a fixed-length concatenation of the global and per-path features (a fixed path count), and a
\emph{scoring} version implementing the permutation-equivariant, mask-aware design of
\cref{sec:p2design:scoring} with a shared per-path actor and a Deep Sets masked-mean-pool critic. The
hierarchical training loop runs the two agents at their respective cadences, storing each agent's
transitions in its own replay buffer and crediting the application agent over the window of frames its
bitrate governed.

\paragraph{VMAF surrogate.} The quality term of \cref{eq:p2qoe} needs a mapping from an encoding (and
network conditions) to perceptual quality. Two are supported: a default bitrate-only logarithmic curve fit
through published quality anchors, and a learned surrogate that interpolates a
quality-of-service$\rightarrow$VMAF table over bitrate, loss, delay, and jitter (produced by a sibling
data-generation project). When the learned surrogate is active, the explicit loss penalty in
\cref{eq:p2qoe} is dropped to avoid double-counting the loss it already reflects.

\subsection{Known Gaps}
\label{sec:p2impl:gaps}

The current testbed has five limitations that scope the results of \cref{sec:mp:eval} and define concrete
follow-up work.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Transport realism.} Subflows are modeled with TCP-like congestion control rather than true
  Multipath QUIC; this preserves congestion, queueing, and loss dynamics but masks some network loss as
  latency, and forgoes QUIC's stream multiplexing, connection migration, and unreliable datagrams.
  Swapping in a real QUIC module is future work.
  \item \textbf{Surrogate coverage.} The learned VMAF surrogate's loss, delay, and jitter axes are
  currently under-sampled, so the quality term is effectively bitrate-dominated; a richer surrogate would
  activate those axes and let quality respond to delivery conditions directly. Every result in
  \cref{sec:mp:eval} therefore uses the bitrate-only logarithmic curve.
  \item \textbf{Training body.} Every policy in this chapter except the arm of
  \cref{sec:p2eval:ns3native} is trained on the mock backend and evaluated on both, because an
  NS-3-native training seed costs ${\approx}$\,23\,h against the mock's ${\approx}$\,1.5\,h and NS-3 runs
  cannot be parallelized. That the system \emph{learns} on the packet-level body is now established at
  three seeds and not merely supported, but the arm that establishes it is unconditioned, so every
  conditioning, transfer, and mid-call result here remains mock-trained --- and mock-trained policies
  transfer in a way mock evaluation cannot predict (\cref{sec:p2eval:ns3}).
  \item \textbf{Episode independence.} Episodes reset in-band to keep the shared-memory bridge alive,
  which leaves congestion state warm across resets, so episodes are not independent draws.
  \item \textbf{Horizon-agnosticism is a design property, not a measured one.} The application agent
  never observes how far along the call is (\cref{sec:p2design:hierarchy}), so nothing in the policy can
  key on a horizon; but every episode this chapter reports is \SI{30}{\second} long, so the property is
  established by construction rather than by a long-call measurement. A single multi-minute rollout with
  the policy unchanged would convert it into one.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Evaluation: Regimes of Value}
\label{sec:mp:eval}

This section evaluates the multipath system and answers nine questions in turn.
\Cref{sec:p2eval:setup} fixes the setup, the nine policies compared, and the statistical protocol.
\Cref{sec:p2eval:results} asks whether the learned pair beats every baseline, and under which
application intents, and \cref{sec:p2eval:head} asks what produces that margin --- establishing that
the permutation-equivariant path head is load-bearing rather than convenient, and measuring the
path-count independence it was adopted for. \Cref{sec:p2eval:regimes} asks the question the chapter is
named for --
\emph{when} does learned scheduling earn its keep -- and answers it with a mechanism rather than a
scenario label. \Cref{sec:p2eval:transfer} asks whether the three intents produce genuinely different
policies or merely relabel the reward, and \cref{sec:p2eval:conditioning} asks the thesis's own
question of this system: whether one policy conditioned on the intent can replace the three that bake it
in. \Cref{sec:p2eval:switch} then asks the question the fixed-intent measurements structurally cannot,
whether conditioning is \emph{necessary} rather than merely harmless when the application changes its
intent mid-connection. \Cref{sec:p2eval:ns3} asks whether any of it survives \emph{transfer} to a packet-level transport
stack --- the answer is that it survives for half the training seeds and mock score cannot say which
half --- and \cref{sec:p2eval:ns3native} asks the question that leaves open, whether the system learns
on that stack when trained there. \Cref{sec:p2eval:ablation} isolates the remaining design choices,
including the ones that did not pay off, and \cref{sec:p2eval:seeds} draws
together what the section's retractions have in common, which is a result in its own right.

Every figure in this section is rendered from evaluation runs against checkpoints already on disk. Two
results required new training rather than new evaluation, and both are identified where they appear: the
one conditioning arm of \cref{sec:p2eval:conditioning}, and the three packet-level-native seeds of
\cref{sec:p2eval:ns3native}.

\subsection{Setup, Baselines, and Statistical Protocol}
\label{sec:p2eval:setup}

\paragraph{Scenarios.} The \emph{dynamic} scenario is the headline test: six candidate paths, none
individually sufficient, with a latency trap, a shared-bottleneck pair, and all four non-stationary
mechanisms of \cref{sec:p2impl:dataplane} enabled, so that the identity and quality of the best paths
change during a call. The \emph{static} scenario is a three-path stationary topology retained as a
contrast case; \cref{sec:p2eval:regimes} explains why it is the less informative of the two.

\paragraph{Metric.} The primary metric is mean per-window QoE (\cref{eq:p2qoe}) under the intent being
evaluated; we also report the underlying achieved bitrate, latency, loss, and VMAF. Because each intent
carries its own reward, QoE is comparable \emph{within} an intent and never across intents --- all
comparisons below are read down a column.

\paragraph{Baselines and ablations.} Nine policies are compared. Five are fixed scheduling rules
driving a common reactive bitrate controller, so that the comparison isolates \emph{scheduling}:
\emph{even} split over live paths, \emph{single} highest-throughput path, \emph{proportional} allocation
by recent per-path throughput, \emph{random} (a fresh Dirichlet draw per frame), and \emph{minRTT}, the
default scheduler in essentially every Multipath TCP and Multipath QUIC implementation. Under dynamics
all five are mask-aware, operating only over active paths, so they are not handicapped by churn they
cannot see. minRTT is included because the other four are rules we wrote, and a learned scheduler should
be measured against the rule the field actually deploys: it fills paths greedily in ascending smoothed
RTT, each up to its congestion window, which is the per-frame form of the reference packet-level rule.
Collapsing it to ``send everything on the lowest-RTT path'' would make it a relabelled \emph{single} and
would understate it, since real minRTT spreads once a window binds. A sixth, \emph{WebRTC}, is the
strongest external comparison point: a stateful Google Congestion Control bitrate estimator driving a
proportional split, i.e.\ what a deployed real-time video stack does today. Two ablations decompose the learned pair itself: \emph{app-only} keeps the learned bitrate
controller and replaces the transport agent with an even split, and \emph{path-only} keeps the learned
split and replaces the application agent with the same GCC estimator. Together the two ablations answer
which half of the hierarchy carries the result.

Two standard rules are absent. BLEST~\cite{Ferlin2016}
and ECF~\cite{Lim2017} are the research refinements of minRTT, both addressing its head-of-line-blocking
failure mode by predicting whether sending on a slower path will delay completion. Each depends on its
own send-time model, which would have to be implemented faithfully rather than approximated from the
quantities already in our observation, and we did not implement them; the comparison against minRTT is
therefore against the deployed default and not against the best published fixed rule. The two learned
schedulers closest to this work, ReLeS~\cite{Zhang2019} and Peekaboo~\cite{Wu2020}, are likewise
discussed in \cref{sec:rel:transport} but not run here, since neither co-optimizes the offered load and
so neither can be dropped into this system's place.

\paragraph{Statistical protocol.} Every comparison is graded over a grid of training checkpoints
$\times$ evaluation seeds, five episodes per cell, with all policies seeing identical conditions within
a cell. Three quantities accompany every claim: the 95\% confidence interval over evaluation seeds, the
\emph{training-seed spread} $\mathit{SD}_{\mathrm{train}}$ (the standard deviation of per-checkpoint
mean QoE), and a per-cell ranking-stability check that reports whether the learned pair is first in
\emph{every} cell rather than merely on average. The middle quantity is the one that matters, and it
deserves stating as a methodological position rather than a footnote: a margin smaller than
$\mathit{SD}_{\mathrm{train}}$ is a statement about which training seed was drawn, not about the method.
Adopting it forced us to retract or narrow four verdicts we had previously drawn from single-checkpoint
evaluations; every such reversal is reported in place below, and \cref{sec:p2eval:seeds} draws out what
they have in common.

\paragraph{What that rule is, and what it is not.} ``Margin larger than
$\mathit{SD}_{\mathrm{train}}$'' gates every verdict in this section, so it should be stated for what it
is: a deliberately conservative \emph{screening heuristic}, not a significance test, and it has one
known weakness. An $\mathit{SD}$ estimated from three
training seeds is itself uncertain enough that the threshold moves between arms and between seed
counts --- \cref{sec:p2eval:conditioning} reports a spread moving noisily and in both directions when
the seed count doubles from three to six --- so the rule screens claims rather than certifying them,
and a reader who took it for a statistic would correctly object that $n = 3$ supports no such
inference. What it does supply is a threshold that is hard to game and that no result in this chapter
clears by accident: adopting it is what caught the four retractions of \cref{sec:p2eval:seeds}. Where a
verdict turns on it we give the per-seed means as well, as \cref{tab:p2seeds} does, so that a reader may
apply their own threshold; and \cref{sec:p2eval:conditioning} reports the minimum effect this design
can resolve at all, which is the quantity that bounds every null result below.

honest reading: on this scenario the rate half matters more than the split half for a non-learning
system.

the design's question are different. Against what a deployed real-time video stack does today ---
a Google Congestion Control bitrate estimator~\cite{Carlucci2016} over a proportional split --- the

swaps only the bitrate controller, it is worth $+0.061$ to $+0.133$, which is a statement about the

\texttt{presenter}, so the apparent inversion is a statement about how variable delivered latency is
under the tightest deadline rather than about the ordering. What the intent buys is therefore visible on
quality and rate at this resolution and not on delivered latency, and
\cref{sec:p2eval:conditioning} recovers the latency trend where it is measurable: over the continuous
intent sweep, which averages eleven operating points rather than three, latency rises with the intent
parameter in both conditioned arms. The conditioned policy reproduces the bitrate and quality ordering
\emph{continuously}, as a function of the intent it is handed, rather than in three discrete steps.

share returns when the path does.

\paragraph{The same claim, measured over the grid.} A figure of one episode is an illustration, so we
recomputed it over all 81 evaluation cells, and doing so separates a statistic that cannot fail from
two that can. The share allocated to a path while it is down is \emph{exactly} zero, over 35{,}433
down-path frames --- but that measures the masked softmax, which excludes inactive paths by
construction (\cref{sec:p2design:scoring}), not the policy. It confirms the liveness mask is wired
through end to end and is worth nothing as behavioral evidence.

What the architecture does \emph{not} force is whether the scheduler takes a recovered path back, and
where it puts the freed bytes meanwhile. Measuring both against each path's own pre-outage share ---
normalized by an even split over the live set, since with fewer paths live every share inflates
mechanically --- gives one positive and one negative. Across 123 churn events per intent, a returning
path is restored to 90\% of its pre-outage share within \textbf{three frames} (\SI{0.1}{\second}) in
83--89\% of events, and ends 30 frames later slightly \emph{above} where it started
($1.02$--$1.11\times$). Nothing forces that: a policy that quietly abandoned recovered paths would read
zero here forever and would be wasting capacity invisibly. But the redistribution during an outage is
\emph{not} delay-aware at the intents that price delay most. The correlation between a live path's gain
in share and its negated smoothed RTT is $+0.075$ $[0.130]$ under \texttt{interactive} and $+0.016$
$[0.125]$ under \texttt{presenter}, both inside their own training-seed spread; only \texttt{passive}
clears it, at $+0.221$ $[0.047]$. The freed bytes are spread roughly in proportion to what was already
there rather than steered toward the faster paths. That is a real limitation, and it is the same
quantity --- delay-weighted allocation --- that \cref{sec:p2eval:ns3} finds decides whether a policy
transfers to the packet-level backend.


\subsection{Why the Permutation-Equivariant Head Is Load-Bearing}
\label{sec:p2eval:head}
\label{sec:p2eval:pathcount}

Before asking when the learned pair earns its margin, we establish what produces it. Scoring each
candidate with shared weights is usually presented as the way to accommodate a variable action set ---
a convenience. On this problem it is the single largest measured effect in the thesis, and without it
the result of \cref{sec:p2eval:results} does not exist at all.

\paragraph{The permutation-equivariant path head is what makes the system work.} Replacing the scoring
transport agent of \cref{sec:p2design:scoring} with the legacy flat MLP over a fixed-length
concatenation --- the two configurations are field-identical apart from that one architectural key ---
costs $0.231 \pm 0.029$ QoE, positive in all nine evaluation cells, against training-seed spreads of
0.013 and 0.037. This is the largest effect measured anywhere in this chapter --- six to eighteen times
the two spreads it is measured against --- and its importance is not the size of the gap but where the
gap lands: at 0.386
the flat agent is beaten by WebRTC and by the single-path baseline in \emph{every one} of the nine
cells, so \textbf{with the flat head, the headline claim of this chapter is false} --- the learned pair
ranks first in $0/9$ cells rather than $9/9$. The dynamic-input architecture is therefore not a
convenience for handling a variable path count; it is load-bearing. Isolating the scheduler makes the
mechanism cleaner still: the path-only ablation, which is exactly this head under a fixed GCC bitrate
driver, moves by $0.401 \pm 0.039$.

Two limits bound it honestly. This is the architecture's home turf --- the dynamic scenario has churn,
so the live path set changes within an episode and the flat MLP must encode a varying-cardinality set
into fixed slots, which means the result establishes necessity \emph{under churn} and says nothing about
the static scenarios. And the flat arm's own training-seed spread is roughly three times the scoring
arm's, so part of the gap is that the flat learner is less consistent rather than only worse on average;
at three seeds those are not separable.

The gap is also not bought with parameters. Loading the two checkpoints directly, the scoring policy
carries 69.1\,k parameters against the flat policy's 77.8\,k at the nominal six paths, and its critic
140.3\,k against 155.1\,k --- 11\% fewer in both cases. The margin is smaller than the same inversion in
\cref{sec:p1eval:ablation}, where the fixed-action head carries 85.3\,k against the scoring agents'
36.2--37.5\,k, and the multipath comparison should be quoted on its own numbers rather than that one.
The structural difference is the one that matters and it is not visible at $N = 6$: the flat policy's
first layer is $256 \times (4 + 5N)$ and its two output heads are $N \times 256$ each, so it grows by
about 1.8\,k parameters per additional path, while the scoring policy is 69,122 parameters at every path
count from 2 to 96, as the sweep below measures. A shared per-path encoder is evaluated once per
candidate and reused across all of them, where a fixed head must spend output capacity on every slot it
might ever need. The dynamic-input design is therefore cheaper to store as well as better to run, and
its cost is paid instead in inference time, which grows linearly in the path count and which we
measure below to be far inside the frame budget.

\paragraph{Permutation equivariance holds to float32 precision on trained policies.} The scoring head
is permutation-equivariant by construction, and \cref{ch:single_path} backs its discrete counterpart
with a 483840-trial invariance test rather than an assertion. The same measurement here: three trained
checkpoints $\times$ 200 on-distribution decision contexts $\times$ 60 random permutations, for
\textbf{36{,}000 trials}. Permutations are drawn over the whole path set, so the liveness mask is
carried along with the rows --- the case that matters under churn, and the one a fixed-slot
architecture cannot satisfy. Relabelling the paths permutes the split and changes nothing else: the
largest deviation in any component of any split is $4.9\times10^{-7}$. The Deep Sets critic is
correspondingly invariant, its largest deviation $1.5\times10^{-5}$ on values of $|Q| \approx 74$, which
is $10^{-5}$ of the spread of $Q$ across contexts. Both are float32 round-off, which is the expected
answer; the point of measuring is that it is now an answer rather than a claim.

The permutation-equivariant head exists so that one policy serves any number of paths, with a parameter
count independent of $N$ and inference linear in $N$. \Cref{sec:p2design:scoring} asserts that by
construction, and the assertion is cheap to make and easy to get wrong. We measure it here, as the
continuous counterpart of \cref{fig:p1eval:pathcount}. Nothing here is retrained: every number comes
from the same six-path checkpoints used throughout the chapter, loaded unmodified into environments with
between two and twelve live paths.

\paragraph{Holding capacity fixed, because otherwise the sweep measures bandwidth.} Truncating or
extending the six-path topology of \cref{tab:app:p2topo} would confound path count with aggregate
capacity: QoE would rise with $N$ because there is more bandwidth to go around, and the curve would say
nothing about the architecture. The primary sweep therefore holds the aggregate at the nominal
\SI{15.5}{\mega\bit\per\second} at every $N$, so that $N$ varies the \emph{granularity} of the
allocation decision and not the \emph{amount} of resource. Each configuration reuses the six role
templates of \cref{tab:app:p2topo} verbatim --- clean low-RTT, clean, latency trap, congested, and the
shared-bottleneck pair --- in that order of importance for $N < 6$, cycling with scaled delays for
$N > 6$, so the cross-traffic fractions stay in their nominal 0.30--0.55 range throughout. The minimum
live-path count scales as $\lceil N/2 \rceil$ to preserve the nominal half-of-six fraction, and a
shared-bottleneck group exists once per completed cycle of six, so $N = 2, 3, 4$ carry none. The $N = 6$
configuration is byte-identical to \cref{tab:app:p2topo}'s, which is the anchor check: it reproduces the
chapter's own scoring arm to four decimals. \Cref{app:p2pathcount} gives the construction in full.

\paragraph{The architectural claim holds from four paths up, and the scheduler's value grows with
$N$.} \Cref{fig:p2eval:pathcount}(a) reports the sweep. The checkpoint loads and runs at every $N$
without retraining or padding, and the learned pair ranks first in \textbf{all nine cells at $N = 4$, 6,
8, and 12} --- 36 of 36 --- with a margin over the best hand-written policy of $+0.081$, $+0.070$,
$+0.068$, and $+0.060$ against training-seed spreads of 0.013--0.021. That is the by-construction claim
turned into a measurement.

Two further readings come with it, and one is a new positive result. The transport agent's contribution
\emph{grows with the path count}: measured as in \cref{sec:p2eval:regimes}, the QoE the scheduler adds
over an even split runs 0.001, 0.209, 0.208, 0.462, 0.640, and 0.732 at $N = 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12$. (As a
recovery ratio the same numbers read 99.8\%, 66.3\%, 67.3\%, 25.1\%, $-8.2$\%, and $-38.1$\% --- and go
negative at eight paths, which is the same breakdown of the ratio that
\cref{sec:p2eval:regimes} runs into.) At two paths,
holding the total fixed gives two fat paths and an even split is already near-optimal, so disabling the
scheduler costs nothing measurable; at twelve, an even split places \SI{1.3}{\mega\bit\per\second} on
each and the ablation goes \emph{negative}. This is the headroom-homogeneity axis of
\cref{sec:p2eval:regimes} traversed by a variable the scenario file does not otherwise expose, and it is
the cleanest demonstration of that mechanism in the chapter. The second reading is where the ranking
breaks and why: at $N = 2$ the learned pair is first in only 4 of 9 cells and at $N = 3$ in 8 of 9. With
two fat paths there is almost no scheduling decision to make, so the learned split's extra degrees of
freedom buy nothing while its higher bitrate costs something. $N = 2$ is the lower edge of the
architecture's useful range rather than a failure of it --- it is the regime in which the scheduler was
not needed. QoE peaks at $N = 4$ and declines to $N = 12$ by 0.104, which is expected under a fixed
total: more paths at constant capacity means thinner paths, more churn events per episode, and more ways
for a share to land on a dying one.

\paragraph{The flat head cannot be evaluated off its training path count at all.} The same load test
run against the flat checkpoints fails at every $N \neq 6$, with a hard shape error at \emph{both} ends
of the network: at $N = 12$ the first layer is $256 \times 34$ in the checkpoint against $256 \times 64$
in the model, because the flat observation's \emph{width} is a function of $N$, and the two output heads
are $6 \times 256$ against $12 \times 256$. Padding to a fixed maximum path count would paper over the
output layer and not over the input. This sharpens the ablation above: the flat head does not merely
score 0.231 lower on the six-path scenario, it cannot be run on any other path count. The scoring
policy, by contrast, is 69,122 parameters at every $N$ from 2 to 96 --- the parameter-independence claim
measured rather than asserted.

\paragraph{Path-count invariance is not capacity invariance.} A contrast sweep holds each path's rate at
its nominal value instead, so aggregate capacity scales with $N$ from \SI{5.5}{\mega\bit\per\second} at
two paths to \SI{31}{\mega\bit\per\second} at twelve (\cref{fig:p2eval:pathcountperpath}). The two
sweeps disagree at small $N$, and the disagreement is the result. Under the fixed total the policy is
fine at $N = 2$ (0.577, first in 4 of 9 cells); under fixed per-path rates it \emph{collapses} to
$-0.483$, first in 0 of 9, with a training-seed spread of 0.316 --- twenty-four times its nominal
0.013. The variable that separates the two is not the path count but the aggregate capacity: at a third
of the capacity the checkpoints trained on, the application agent keeps asking for a bitrate the network
cannot carry and QoE goes negative for the learned pair and its app-only ablation alike. Above the
training point the policy is untroubled, ranking first in 9 of 9 cells at $N = 8$ and $N = 12$, at
twelve with the tightest spread in either sweep. Path-count invariance is therefore supported and
capacity invariance is refuted; they are separate properties and the first must not be read as implying
the second. The failure mode is the same one \cref{sec:p2eval:regimes} finds on the harsh held-out
environment, reached along a different axis --- in both cases it is the application agent that breaks
first.

\paragraph{The per-frame inference cost fits the frame budget with three orders of magnitude to
spare.} The transport agent is the design's one hard real-time component: it runs once per frame, so
its inference must complete well inside \SI{33}{\milli\second} at 30\,fps.
\Cref{fig:p2eval:decision} measures it across methods. Pooled over the 40{,}500 calls of a full
nine-cell grading at six paths, the learned split decision costs \SI{0.536}{\milli\second} on average on
a 16-core CPU host with no GPU, tightly distributed across frames --- about 1.6\% of the frame budget,
and roughly $43\times$ the cost of the cheapest fixed rule (even split, at \SI{0.0124}{\milli\second}),
which is nonetheless an irrelevant difference at this scale.
Over the same pool the 99th percentile of the split decision is
\SI{0.713}{\milli\second} and the 99.9th under \SI{3.6}{\milli\second}, so the tail that a real-time
claim actually rests on stays 10--45$\times$ inside the budget. The application agent's bitrate decision
is cheaper still and runs 30 times less often. The paragraph after next measures how that cost scales with the
path count, and reports the one part of the distribution that does not fit comfortably.

\paragraph{Decision cost is a large constant plus a small term in $N$, which corrects this chapter's own
extrapolation.} \Cref{fig:p2eval:pathcount}(b) measures the split decision at
$N \in \{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12\}$ inside full episodes and at
$N \in \{6, 12, 24, 48, 96\}$ in an isolated micro-benchmark, both with warm-up discarded and both
timing the same span the chapter times elsewhere. The two agree. Least squares over
$N \in [2, 96]$ gives
\begin{equation}
t(N) = 0.483 + 0.00501\,N\ \text{ms}, \qquad R^2 = 0.990,
\label{eq:p2decisionfit}
\end{equation}
so the cost is indeed linear in the path count --- but \textbf{94\% of it at six paths is the constant},
and the marginal cost of a path is \SI{5.0}{\micro\second}. The frame budget is therefore reached near
$N \approx 6500$, not the ${\approx}200$ this chapter previously inferred from the six-path mean by
assuming the cost was proportional to $N$. The correction is in the
conservative direction and the right sentence is not ``linear in $N$'' but ``a fixed
${\approx}\SI{0.48}{\milli\second}$ plus \SI{5}{\micro\second} per path''.

The tail deserves stating separately, because a real-time claim is a claim about tails. In-episode the
99th percentile is \SIrange{0.68}{0.86}{\milli\second} at every $N$, and over the micro-benchmark's
wider range it has no measurable dependence on the path count at all: the same least-squares fit applied
to the 99th percentile returns $R^2 = 0.058$, so its extrapolated crossover is not a number worth
quoting. The 99.9th percentile is \SIrange{3.2}{3.6}{\milli\second}, still tenfold inside the budget.
The \emph{extreme} tail is not: at every sweep point, $N = 2$
included, the slowest single call is \SIrange{170}{219}{\milli\second} and about 0.01\% of frames exceed
\SI{33.3}{\milli\second}. That rate does not grow with $N$ and appears identically in the isolated
benchmark at a twentieth of the sample count, which is what one expects of a Python-runtime artifact ---
allocation and garbage collection --- rather than of model cost. It will not be fixed by shrinking the
network, and a deployment would need a pre-allocated inference path to remove it. A deployment claim
should quote the 99th percentile \emph{and} disclose the one-in-$10^4$ overrun, not the mean alone.

\paragraph{What bounds this.} Three limits. The measurement is on the analytical backend, so
\cref{sec:p2eval:ns3}'s decorrelation means none of it is evidence about the packet-level body at a
different $N$. The checkpoints are the six-path ones throughout, so this measures \emph{transfer} across
$N$ --- which is the architectural claim --- and not what the head would achieve if trained at each $N$.
And $N = 2, 3, 4$ carry no shared-bottleneck group, so their difficulty differs from the larger counts
along an axis other than the path count.

\subsection{When Does Splitting Help? The Governing Quantity}
\label{sec:p2eval:regimes}

The chapter's title promises regimes of value, and the honest form of that claim is not ``learned
scheduling helps when the network is dynamic.'' It is sharper, and it is mechanical. The quantity that
governs whether a scheduler has anything to do is the relationship between the \emph{share an even
split places on each path} and the \emph{residual capacity of the weakest path}. Where every path can
absorb its even share, any allocation works and the learned agent has nothing to earn. Where some path
cannot, the split decides whether the frame arrives. Volatility matters because churn, regime shifts,
and bursts are mechanisms that \emph{destroy} that headroom homogeneity transiently --- but they are not
the only ones, and naming them as the axis mistakes a cause for the variable.

Two observations support the reframing, and both cut against the simpler story.

\paragraph{A static network is not a safe network.} On the static three-path scenario, the app-only
ablation does not merely lose --- it collapses to $-0.396$ QoE at \SI{696}{\milli\second} latency and
52\% loss, because the topology is \emph{asymmetric} (8/4/\SI{2}{\mega\bit\per\second}) and an even
split overdrives the weakest path continuously. Staticness bought it nothing. Conversely, a static
topology of four \emph{near-equal} paths puts app-only within 2\% of the full system. It is the
homogeneity of headroom, not the stationarity of the network, that decides.

\paragraph{The static scenario cannot discriminate at all.} On that same three-path scenario the
learned pair scores 0.700 against 0.691 for the single-best-path baseline --- a margin of 0.009 against
an $\mathit{SD}_{\mathrm{train}}$ of 0.017. The learned pair is first in six of nine cells and loses all
three cells of one training seed. The correct reading is not that scheduling is unnecessary when paths
are stable; it is that this scenario \emph{cannot tell}, because the margin is smaller than the spread
between training runs of the same method. A practitioner benchmarking a multipath scheduler only on a
stable topology would conclude it does not work, and would be measuring their own seed draw.

\paragraph{Intent strictness is the second axis.} The app-only ablation recovers 35\%, 61\%, and 84\%
of the full system's QoE under \texttt{interactive}, \texttt{presenter}, and \texttt{passive}
respectively (\cref{tab:p2results}). Holding the network fixed and varying only what the application
asks for, the scheduler's contribution more than doubles as the delay budget tightens. This is a
mechanism-level result rather than an average: the stricter the intent, the less slack there is to
absorb a bad allocation, so the more the allocation matters. It is also the point at which this chapter
rejoins \cref{ch:single_path} --- the value of the control machinery is itself a function of the intent.
The trend is one of the few in this chapter that reproduced without qualification when the experiment
was rerun at three training seeds: the single-seed version of the same measurement read 39/55/81\%.

\paragraph{The two axes, measured together.} The two paragraphs above vary one axis each. Varying both
at once turns them into a map, and the map says something neither marginal does.
\Cref{fig:p2eval:regimemap} sweeps the network's harshness against the three intents: five network
regimes (rows) $\times$ three intents (columns), nine evaluation cells each, 135 in total, all
evaluation-only against the specialists of \cref{tab:p2results}. The rows interpolate the six
\texttt{dynamics:} parameters --- churn, regime-shift, burst, and correlated-failure rates and
intensities --- between the two held-out configurations \texttt{dynamic\_ood\_\{mild,harsh\}.yaml},
holding topology, video, and reward fixed, so the only thing that varies down a column is the dynamics
and the nominal row reproduces \cref{tab:p2results} exactly. The row coordinate is not that parameter
bundle but a \emph{measured} consequence of it: the mean loss fraction the \emph{even} baseline suffers
in that cell, which is the direct observation of ``does an even split overdrive the weakest live path''.

The planned cell value was the app-only recovery \emph{ratio} used in the two paragraphs above, and it
does not survive the grid: it is interpretable only while both QoE means are positive, and in the harsh
row they are not, so two negatives divide to $+26.8$ on one cell and the learned pair itself falls below
the even-split floor. Every normalized variant fails the same way. The map therefore reports the
absolute contribution $\mathrm{QoE}(\text{learned}) - \mathrm{QoE}(\text{app-only})$, which is defined
everywhere and is the quantity the ratio was standing in for. That substitution bounds the ratio wherever this chapter quotes it, including the 35/61/84\% above: a recovery ratio is a usable summary only inside the regime where the system works.

\paragraph{The predicted shape holds, and the two axes are not separable.} The contribution rises
monotonically down every column --- by $14\times$, $27\times$, and $17\times$ from the mild to the harsh
environment --- and monotonically across every row from \texttt{passive} to \texttt{interactive} in the
four rows where the system still works. That is the two-axis version of the mechanism this section
argues for, and it is measured rather than inferred from two separate sweeps. What the map adds is that
the axes \emph{interact}: the spread across the three intents grows from 0.039 QoE on the mild
environment to 0.288 at nominal to 0.566 on the harsh one, a factor of 14.5. Intent strictness barely
matters when the network is benign, because an even split is survivable however tight the deadline, and
dominates when it is harsh. An additive reading of the two marginals would be wrong, and a practitioner
who measures the intent axis on a well-behaved network will conclude the intent hardly matters.

\paragraph{Where the system breaks.} The same map bounds the chapter's headline claim, and this is the
more important half of it. Ranking stability degrades down the rows: the learned pair is first in 9 of 9
cells at every intent on the mild and mild--nominal environments, 8/9, 9/9, and 9/9 at nominal, and then
1/9, 5/9, and 6/9 one step below --- and on the harsh held-out environment \textbf{0 of 9, 0 of 9, and 3
of 9}. There its mean QoE is negative at two of three intents ($-0.369$ under \texttt{interactive},
$-0.039$ under \texttt{presenter}), while plain WebRTC-style GCC scores $+0.305$, $+0.353$, and $+0.437$
and beats it in all three columns, as does the \emph{path-only} ablation. That the ablation which keeps
our scheduler and drops our bitrate controller is the one that survives identifies which half breaks:
the application agent keeps asking for a bitrate the network cannot carry. The training-seed spread
rises with it, from 0.018--0.024 in the mild row to 0.051--0.339 in the harsh one, so the learner is not
merely worse outside its training distribution but \emph{inconsistent} outside it.

The honest form of \cref{sec:p2eval:results}'s claim is therefore bounded rather than universal: the
learned pair beats every comparison point on the network it trained on \emph{and} on a milder one it
never saw, and loses to a hand-written heuristic on a harsher one. This is the same wall
\cref{sec:p2eval:ablation}'s domain-randomization arm meets from the other direction, where the harsh
held-out environment turned both arms negative; the map adds a per-cell ranking behind that observation
and locates where along the axis the reversal happens, which is between the nominal environment and one
whose even-split loss fraction is 25\% higher.

\subsection{Do the Intents Produce Different Policies?}
\label{sec:p2eval:transfer}

\Cref{tab:p2results} shows that policies trained under different intents behave differently, but that is
also consistent with the three intents simply being easier or harder to satisfy. The discriminating test
is to evaluate every intent-trained policy under every intent's reward and read down each column: if the
intents produce genuinely specialized policies, each column should be won by its own diagonal entry.
\Cref{tab:p2transfer} reports it. This is the multipath counterpart of the intent-alignment matrix of
\cref{fig:p1eval:heatmap}, and unlike that one it does not come out clean --- in a way that is more
interesting than a clean failure would have been.

Read naively, the table says the \texttt{interactive}-trained policy is the best policy for every
intent, winning all three columns and beating two diagonals on their own terms. Read against the
training-seed spread, it says something considerably more deflationary. The interactive diagonal wins

loses its column by $0.016$, about $0.6\sigma$. The passive diagonal loses by $0.009$, about
$0.3\sigma$. \emph{Every margin in the matrix is inside the spread between training runs of the same
method.} The conclusion is not that one specialist dominates but that, at three training seeds and this budget, \textbf{which intent a policy was trained for does not measurably determine which
intent it is best at}.

This overturns a cleaner-looking result we had previously drawn from the same experiment run at one
training seed per cell, where two of three diagonals held and the third failed conspicuously --- the
\texttt{passive} policy being dominated by the \texttt{presenter} policy on passive's own terms, which
invited an appealing mechanistic story about a weight ($b = 0.1$) too small to generate the gradient
that would train against it. The passive diagonal does still lose. But so does presenter's, and neither
loss clears noise, so the story is not supported: what looked like a specific pathology of relaxed
objectives is one of three statistically indistinguishable outcomes. \Cref{sec:p2eval:seeds} returns to
this, because it is the third time in this chapter that a single-seed result did not survive being run
three times.

The consequence for the design is the one worth carrying forward, and it cuts in an unexpected
direction. The per-intent model zoo that \cref{ch:single_path} argues against is, on this evidence, not
even buying what it charges for: three separately trained specialists at three times the training cost
are not measurably specialized. That \emph{weakens} the motivation for specialists and correspondingly
\emph{strengthens} the case for a single conditioned policy, which now has less to lose than the
single-seed reading suggested. It also fixes the bar. A conditioned policy must clear not the diagonal
but the best entry in each column --- $0.635$, $0.735$, $0.818$ --- since those are the best policies
actually available per intent, whichever intent produced them.

\subsection{One Conditioned Policy, or Three Specialists?}
\label{sec:p2eval:conditioning}

intent as an input replace the family of policies that bake it in? We train two conditioned arms, both
over intents randomized per episode, differing only in \emph{where} the intent is routed ---
\cref{sec:p2design:cancel}'s question made experimental. The \emph{per-path} arm supplies the intent to
the global block and to every per-path feature row; the \emph{global-only} arm supplies it to the global
block alone, and is the control that \cref{prop:p2cancel} predicts should be inert. Each arm is one
policy graded at all three intents against the \cref{tab:p2transfer} bar.

\paragraph{One policy does the work of three, at no measurable cost.} The global-only arm matches the
best specialist under all three intents, with no margin distinguishable from zero and two of the three
nominally positive. The per-path arm matches under \texttt{passive} and \texttt{presenter} and carries a
persistent shortfall under \texttt{interactive} --- which has a cause, and it is not the conditioning
scheme: \cref{tab:p2fixednorm} below closes it. This is the multipath analogue of
\cref{sec:p1eval:intent}'s result and it is the chapter's answer to the thesis's central question --- but
it must be stated as \emph{parity}, not superiority. A failure to detect a difference at this spread is
not a demonstration of equivalence, and the claim the chapter makes is the weaker, defensible one:
\emph{a single conditioned policy replaces three specialists without measurable loss}, at a third of the
training cost and with one artifact to deploy rather than three.

\paragraph{The resolution of that null, stated.} A null is only a claim alongside the effect size it
could have detected, so we compute it. Treating the training seed as the unit of replication --- which
is what $\mathit{SD}_{\mathrm{train}}$ is for --- with six seeds per arm, the pooled spreads of
\cref{tab:p2intent}, $\alpha = 0.05$ and 80\% power, the minimum detectable effect is
\SIrange{0.063}{0.087}{} QoE across the three intents.

\emph{Every effect this chapter is asking about is smaller than that.} The specialist margins in
\cref{tab:p2transfer} span 0.002--0.048, and the largest conditioned-versus-bar margin in
\cref{tab:p2intent} is 0.048. The reading is therefore not ``the conditioned policy matches the specialists'' but \emph{this experiment could not have resolved the question either way at the
resolution it achieves}, and the parity statement should be read as bounding the difference to under
about 0.07 QoE rather than as evidence of equivalence. That also explains \cref{tab:p2transfer} in the
same breath: the specialists failed to separate from one another for the same reason, so the bar was
noise-limited before the conditioned arms ever met it.

Two things follow that are worth more than the null itself. First, the fix is not more seeds but a
\emph{paired} design: the effect that \cref{tab:p2fixednorm} detects, $+0.036$, is well below this
minimum detectable effect and was resolved anyway, because there the two arms are compared cell by cell
on matched environment draws rather than as two independently trained populations. Pairing bought
roughly a factor of two in resolution at no extra compute, and it is the cheaper lesson. Second, it
sets a floor on what any future comparison of conditioning schemes on this scenario needs to be
designed for.

Two caveats belong with it rather than after it. First, the bar itself is noise-limited: the specialists
were not measurably specialized (\cref{tab:p2transfer}), so ``matches the specialists'' is a weaker
statement here than it would be against a strongly separated set. Second, doubling from three to six
training seeds --- the numbers above are the six-seed grading --- did not shrink the spread. The
global-only arm's $\mathit{SD}_{\mathrm{train}}$ moved from 0.062/0.087/0.055 to 0.041/0.057/0.059, that
is, noisily and in both directions. More seeds bought a better estimate of the mean and did not make the
learner more reliable, which is a property of this learner on this scenario and not a defect of the
experiment.

\paragraph{The control arm did not behave like a control.} \Cref{prop:p2cancel} predicts that the
global-only policy consumes the intent and emits an identical split. It does not. Sweeping the intent
with the decision context frozen, over 200 on-distribution contexts per training seed, the global-only
arm's behavioral divergence is $0.146$ $[0.015]$ against the per-path arm's $0.157$ $[0.048]$ --- a
difference well inside the per-path arm's own spread, where the prediction was that the first number
would be zero. Only 29\% of the global-only arm's logit perturbation is the common mode the softmax
discards; the predicted figure was essentially all of it.

This is the empirical completion of \cref{sec:p2design:cancel}'s second breaker, and it is the more
informative half of the chapter's theory. At random initialization the leak through the shared encoder's
nonlinearity is of order $10^{-4}$ and looks negligible. \emph{Training turns it into the primary
channel}: a network free to route the intent around the cancellation learns to do so, and the softmax's
shift-invariance stops constraining it at all. The proposition is not wrong --- it is exact, for the
function class it names --- but the function class is narrow enough that a deep encoder escapes it
without being designed to.

Read on those numbers alone, per-path conditioning is \emph{not} empirically justified in this system:
it costs 30 additional input dimensions, scores no better on divergence, and scores worse on QoE at two
of three intents. But the chapter's own theory names a suspect for that shortfall, and testing it
changes the verdict.

\paragraph{The shortfall is the normalizer leak, not the conditioning scheme.} Breaker~3 of
\cref{sec:p2design:cancel} is a mechanism specific to the per-path arm. Under per-path conditioning the
intent reaches each path row \emph{twice} --- once as the broadcast feature the design intends, and once
as a $4\times$ rescaling of that row's sRTT column by a latency normalizer that is itself part of the
intent --- so the network must reconcile two correlated channels where the design specifies one. Setting
\texttt{intent.obs\_norm: fixed}, which pins the observation scales to the G.114 and Y.1541 constants and
closes that channel exactly (row span 0.000, last row of \cref{tab:p2cancel}), isolates it. We train a
third arm that way, three seeds at the matched budget, and grade it against the per-path arm on the same
cells; each side is evaluated under the normalizer it trained with, so the delta is paired per training
seed $\times$ evaluation seed. \Cref{tab:p2fixednorm} reports it.

Closing the leak recovers $+0.036 \pm 0.016$ under \texttt{interactive} and $+0.033 \pm 0.012$ under
\texttt{presenter} --- the two intents that carried a deficit, positive in 9 of 9 and 8 of 9 paired cells
--- and nothing measurable under \texttt{passive} ($+0.011$, 5 of 9), which never had one. An effect that
appears exactly where the deficit was and only there is what a mechanism looks like; a difference in
training luck would not respect that pattern. The residual shortfalls, $-0.006$ and $-0.015$, are inside
the pooled training-seed spread and are no longer detectable. Two secondary signs point the same way.
The training-seed spread \emph{tightens}, from 0.041 to 0.024 under \texttt{interactive} and from 0.054
to 0.010 under \texttt{passive} --- the only intervention in this chapter that reduced it, against the
seed count doubling, which did not. And the per-cell ranking improves: under \texttt{interactive} the
learned pair goes from first in 6 of 9 cells to first in 8 of 9.

A direct probe confirms the channel rather than only its consequence. Sweeping the intent with the
decision context frozen admits breakers 1 and 2 alone; rebuilding the whole observation per intent
additionally admits breaker~3. Under \texttt{profile} the two probes differ by $-0.024$ in divergence.
Under \texttt{fixed} they agree \emph{to every printed digit on every training seed}, which is
\cref{tab:p2cancel}'s row-span-0.000 result confirmed end to end on trained policies rather than on a
freshly initialized network. The sign under \texttt{profile} is itself informative: rebuilding the
observation \emph{reduces} measured divergence, so the normalizer channel and the intended per-path
channel partly cancel each other. The per-path arm was not merely carrying a redundant input; it was
spending capacity reconciling two inputs that partly undo one another.

The consequence for the design is that the two conditioning schemes are at \emph{parity} on QoE once the
leak is closed --- \texttt{fixed} scores $0.630 / 0.719 / 0.821$ against the global-only arm's
$0.639 / 0.693 / 0.823$, differences of $-0.010$, $+0.027$, and $-0.002$, all inside the training-seed
spread and mixed in sign. Per-path conditioning is therefore not the empirically inferior option this
section was about to call it; it is the equally good option that carries a guarantee the cheaper one
does not, and the cost measured earlier was an implementation choice rather than a property of the
scheme. That choice was made deliberately and for a good reason --- disclosing the normalizer as an
input is what keeps the per-intent specialists of \cref{tab:p2transfer} a valid baseline
(\cref{app:p2hyper}) --- but any new conditioned arm should fix the normalizers. What survives is an
argument about guarantees that no longer has to be made \emph{against} performance: per-path injection
is the only routing that is guaranteed to work, it costs nothing measurable once its own leak is
closed, and global routing happened to work here through a mechanism nobody specified and nobody can
rely on.

Two limits bound this. It is three training seeds against the six-seed arms of \cref{tab:p2intent}, so
only the \texttt{fixed}-versus-\texttt{profile} delta is properly paired; and the \texttt{interactive}
block remains rank-unstable in both arms, so it is the intent at which this scenario discriminates least
reliably --- which the intervention did not change.

\paragraph{The conditioned policy interpolates across a continuum of intents.} Sweeping the intent
linearly from \texttt{interactive} ($\lambda = 0$) to \texttt{passive} ($\lambda = 1$) in eleven steps,
over all six training seeds of each arm, both arms trade delay for quality smoothly, and, in the pooled mean, monotonically on rate and
quality (\cref{fig:p2eval:intentsweep}); delivered latency is monotone for the global-only arm and not
for the per-path one, which is the subject of the tie-breaker paragraph below.
Achieved bitrate rises from 2379 to \SI{3505}{\kilo\bit\per\second}
(per-path) and 3003 to \SI{3679}{\kilo\bit\per\second} (global-only), with no single step of the sweep
carrying more than a quarter of the trend. Because the intent bundle contains the deadline, the raw
metrics move even for a policy that ignores the intent entirely, so the de-confounded statistic is the
ratio of the learned policy's span to a non-learning control's span over the same rollouts: the
conditioned policies adapt by 11--24$\times$ the control on bitrate and VMAF. What is licensed by this
is that the policy generalizes across a \emph{continuum} of intents rather than memorizing three
operating points --- the counterpart of \cref{sec:p1eval:zeroshot}'s claim, but not the same claim.
Training sampled the intent space continuously, so the interior $\lambda$ are in-distribution; only
$\lambda = -0.2$ and $\lambda = 1.2$ are genuinely unseen, and they fail asymmetrically. At
$\lambda = 1.2$ (deadline \SI{924}{\milli\second}) both arms hold QoE at 0.83 while latency rises to
\SIrange{134}{144}{\milli\second}; at $\lambda = -0.2$ (deadline \SI{56}{\milli\second}) both collapse,
to 0.19 and 0.25, with bitrate falling to ${\approx}\SI{1590}{\kilo\bit\per\second}$. The policies
extrapolate toward looser intents and not toward stricter ones, which is the expected direction, since
$\lambda < 0$ asks for a deadline below anything the network can deliver rather than merely an
unfamiliar one.

\paragraph{The tie-breaker between the two arms does not survive six seeds.} At three training seeds
this sweep read as a third axis on which global-only conditioning was the better arm: Spearman $\rho$
against $\lambda$ was $0.96$ on latency for the global arm against the per-path arm's $0.41$, whose
latency curve climbs and then falls back. At six seeds latency still favors the global arm (0.973
against 0.445), but bitrate and VMAF \emph{reverse} to favor per-path (1.000 and 0.991 against 0.800 and
0.755), where the three-seed reading had them the other way. Decomposing the pooled statistic per
training seed shows why, and it is a failure mode distinct from the ones this chapter has reported so
far: the per-path arm's bitrate trend is positive in all six seeds, while the global arm's is positive
in four and \emph{strongly negative} in two ($\rho = -0.536$ and $-0.982$, training-seed spread 0.789
against the per-path arm's 0.243). Two of six global-only policies raise their bitrate as the intent
gets stricter --- the wrong direction --- and the pooled $\rho$ of 0.800 conceals it completely. Nothing
was under-sampled here; the summary statistic was simply computed over the wrong axis first
(\cref{sec:p2eval:seeds}). The two conditioning schemes are therefore not separable on interpolation quality either, which leaves the 30 saved input dimensions as the only surviving reason
to prefer global-only --- an efficiency argument rather than a behavioral one.

The experiment that would upgrade this from continuum generalization to genuine zero-shot is a third
arm trained on the three anchors alone, with no interior sampling, which is what would make the
interior of the segment unseen; we did not run it, and we therefore claim the weaker property.

\subsection{When the Intent Moves During a Call}
\label{sec:p2eval:switch}

Every result so far --- in this chapter and in \cref{ch:single_path} --- holds the intent fixed for a
whole episode, and that is precisely the regime in which three specialists and one conditioned policy
are interchangeable: a deployment without conditioning simply loads the right specialist at call setup
and pays nothing. If the case for conditioning rested on the fixed-intent measurements alone it would be
an argument about deployment convenience, and a weak one, since \cref{tab:p2intent} can only bound the
quality difference rather than demonstrate it.

The operational advantage exists when the intent moves \emph{mid-connection} --- a call that starts as
screen-share and becomes conversational, or an application that tightens its deadline when the user
starts interacting. Swapping model weights mid-call is a poor answer; taking the intent as an input is
the good one. This subsection is the experiment, and it is the one case the fixed-intent measurements
structurally cannot make: it is where conditioning is \emph{necessary} rather than merely harmless.

  the frame deadline, and the observation normalizers all change at the switch for \emph{every} arm ---
  the world changes for all three --- but only the conditioned policy can observe it, because only its

  \label{fig:p2eval:intentswitch}

The two directions are not symmetric, and only one of them is an argument. \textbf{Relaxing} the
deadline costs a policy that cannot see the change essentially nothing: $+0.020$ QoE for the conditioned
arm, inside its own training-seed spread of $0.042$. That is the expected direction --- a controller
tuned for a strict budget is merely conservative under a loose one, and \cref{fig:p2eval:intentswitch}(a)
shows all three arms tracking each other. \textbf{Tightening} is where conditioning earns its keep. When
the deadline drops from \SI{800}{\milli\second} to \SI{180}{\milli\second}, the specialist still running
the pre-switch policy sees delivered latency spike to ${\approx}\SI{1100}{\milli\second}$ --- six times
the new budget --- and take some ten seconds to drain, while the conditioned policy's stays flat at
${\approx}\SI{50}{\milli\second}$ and never crosses the new deadline at all.

The margin must be read carefully. $+0.321$ QoE over the pre-switch specialist is about $1\sigma$ against
that specialist's own training-seed spread of $0.311$, so it does \emph{not} clear this chapter's
decision rule on the mean. What does clear it is the consistency: the conditioned arm's spread is
$0.035$ against $0.311$ and $0.293$, an order of magnitude tighter, and its \emph{worst} training seed
($+0.556$) beats the pre-switch specialist's mean ($+0.262$) and the post-switch specialist's ($+0.416$).
The defensible claim is about reliability under a mid-call intent change rather than about the average.

The mechanism is visible in the figure and it is the one this chapter has been arguing for. After the
switch the conditioned policy runs a \emph{higher} target bitrate than the pre-switch specialist
(${\approx}2800$ against ${\approx}\SI{2350}{\kilo\bit\per\second}$) while delivering roughly twenty
times lower latency. A higher offered load with lower delivered delay cannot come from rate control; it
has to come from where the bytes are placed. This is the most direct behavioral evidence in the chapter
that the intent reaches the allocation decision and not merely the encoder.

Three limits. This is the mock backend only, because retargeting the intent mid-episode is mock-only by
construction --- the packet-level body takes its deadline as a process-start argument. The switch is a
step rather than a ramp. And the post-switch specialist is a \emph{lower} bound on what instantaneous
weight-swapping would achieve, not an upper one: it lived through the pre-switch half too and carries
the same queue state, which is why it also spikes, to ${\approx}\SI{530}{\milli\second}$, where a call
that had started under the tight deadline would not.

This is the strongest form of the sim-to-real caveat this thesis can state, and it is a methodological
result rather than a caveat on a number. Any procedure that trains $N$ policies on a cheap surrogate,
selects the best by surrogate score, and reports it on the realistic body is --- on this evidence ---
selecting at random, and in the one case we tested would have selected the worst of six. Our own
earlier, single-cell version of this section is exactly that failure mode caught in the act: the cell it
reported was the best evaluation seed of a checkpoint that we can now see is the worst of the six.
Checkpoint selection must happen on the backend the claim is about, or the honest alternative is to
report the distribution over seeds, as \cref{tab:p2ns3,tab:p2seeds} do.

\paragraph{The mechanism: the transferring policies are the ones that keep the network out of queue.}
The split is explicable, and explaining it identifies what the mock is missing. Re-reading the per-frame
traces already stored in the eighteen NS-3 cells --- no new simulation --- the statistic that separates
the two clusters is \emph{delay-weighted allocation}, $\sum_i w_i \cdot \mathrm{sRTT}_i$: where the bytes
actually go, priced by what that path costs at the time. It rank-separates the clusters perfectly, the
three transferring seeds taking the three lowest values, which for a 3--3 split is the strongest
separation six points admit (exact one-tailed permutation $p = 0.050$), and it correlates with NS-3 QoE
at Pearson $r = -0.918$ --- roughly 84\% of the between-seed variance the aggregate table cannot explain.
It factors into two terms that point the same way: the non-transferring policies both run every path
hotter (mean path sRTT \SI{75.8}{\milli\second} against \SI{70.5}{\milli\second}, self-inflicted, since
they drive the higher bitrate into real transport) and aim worse within that (weighting paths slower
than the current average, ratio $1.058$ against $0.997$).

The natural competing hypothesis --- that the clusters differ in loss tolerance --- is refuted by the
same traces: loss-weighted allocation is $0.0137$ against $0.0162$, a gap of $0.28$ standard deviations
and in the wrong direction for two of the six seeds. Split entropy, active-path count, and split
volatility are flat too. Of six behavioral statistics, only the two delay-weighted ones separate.

And this is precisely why the mock cannot predict NS-3. Running the identical analysis on the mock
cells, mean path sRTT is \SI{48}{\milli\second} for all six policies, spanning \SI{1.9}{\milli\second},
where on NS-3 the same six span \SI{9.7}{\milli\second}. The mock's analytical queue does not charge
these policies differently for the queueing they cause, so the one variable that decides the NS-3
outcome is invisible in mock evaluation, and a mock ranking is free to order the seeds however noise
happens to. The $\rho = -0.086$ is not a mysterious variance problem: it is the queue model. The fix is
a higher-fidelity queue in the surrogate, not more surrogate seeds.

\paragraph{How far that mechanism claim reaches.} Three limits bound it, and none is hidden by the
numbers above. The separation rests on $n = 3$ per cluster, and $p = 0.050$ is the \emph{floor}
attainable at a 3--3 split, reached only because the rank separation is perfect --- a single added seed
that breaks the ordering would move it to $p \approx 0.10$, so the statistic is suggestive of a
mechanism rather than load-bearing on its own. Smoothed RTT is also partly endogenous, since loading a
path lengthens that path's own queue, which is why we decompose the statistic rather than quote it
alone; and the least endogenous of its terms, the aiming ratio, is also the weakest ($p = 0.100$).
Finally, this is a behavioral correlation across six trained policies and not an intervention: it does
not establish that forcing a lower delay-weighted allocation would make a non-transferring seed
transfer. The intervention that would --- retraining with a delay-aware split prior, or raising the
delay weight $b$ and re-running --- is the natural next experiment and we did not run it.

\subsection{Training on the Packet-Level Body}
\label{sec:p2eval:ns3native}

Whether the system \emph{learns} on the realistic body, as opposed to transferring to it, is a different
question from the one above, and \cref{tab:p2seeds} makes it the live one rather than a footnote. It is
also the question the previous subsection cannot answer, because every policy in it was trained
somewhere else. Three NS-3-native seeds have since been trained to completion --- 100 episodes each,
identical hyperparameters, the dynamic scenario --- and graded under the protocol this chapter applies
everywhere else.

All three learn, and they learn the same thing. Mean episode QoE over the first ten episodes against the
last ten moves from $0.089$ to $0.480$, from $0.060$ to $0.425$, and from $0.021$ to $0.477$. Over that
same span the offered bitrate \emph{falls}, from ${\approx}\SI{3.0}{\mega\bit\per\second}$ to
\SIrange{2.2}{2.5}{\mega\bit\per\second}, while loss drops from $0.37$ to $0.07$ and one-way latency from
\SI{116}{\milli\second} to \SI{65}{\milli\second}. Against a real queue the policy learns to stop overdriving it --- which is
the delay-weighted-allocation mechanism isolated above, arrived at by training rather than by luck of
the draw.

\paragraph{First in every cell, and the split does not reappear.} \Cref{tab:p2ns3native} is the result
\cref{tab:p2ns3} could not produce. The learned pair leads the whole comparison set by $0.114$ QoE ---

margin of six times its own resolution. Against WebRTC, the strongest policy here that is genuinely
hand-written, the margin is $0.267$. It ranks first in nine of nine cells rather than eight of eighteen. The bimodality of \cref{tab:p2seeds} is gone: per-seed means

2546\,kbit/s) --- the same video pushed through a real transport at a seventh of the loss.

\paragraph{Against mock-trained transfer, on the same cells.} The direct question --- does training on
the packet-level body beat transferring to it? --- is a paired comparison over the identical nine cells,
which cancels the environment noise the two arms share. NS-3-native training wins by $\mathbf{+0.228}$
QoE ($\pm 0.071$ at 95\%), positive in nine of nine cells, smallest cell margin $+0.106$. The two arms
differ in reliability by as much as in level: the mock-trained arm's three per-seed means run from
$0.23$ to $0.46$, a training-seed spread six times the native arm's $0.019$. Where the
mock-trained learned pair fails to top five of its nine cells --- beaten by the path-only ablation in
five and by the app-only ablation in two --- the native arm tops all nine. The 3-of-6 split of
\cref{tab:p2seeds} is therefore a property of the \emph{training body}, not of the method: the same
learner, the same architecture, and the same reward produce a consistent first-place policy once trained
where it is measured.

\paragraph{The mock cannot grade this arm either, and it fails in the opposite direction.} Grading the
three native checkpoints on the mock --- the same nine cells, the analytical backend --- puts the
learned pair \emph{second}, behind WebRTC ($0.518$ against $0.547$), in nine of nine cells, with the
path-only ablation ahead of it in two. Over the nine policies the two backends agree at Spearman
$\rho = 0.467$ (Kendall $\tau = 0.389$, 11 of 36 pairs discordant): WebRTC moves from fourth to first,
minRTT from eighth to fourth, and \texttt{single} from last to fifth. This is the same failure as
\cref{tab:p2seeds}'s $\rho = -0.086$ observed from the other side, and it is the more damaging
direction. The earlier result said the mock cannot pick which mock-trained checkpoint transfers; this
one says the mock, used as a grader, would have rejected the best system this chapter produced and
crowned the deployed baseline instead. Both are the queue-fidelity gap: the surrogate does not charge a
policy for the queueing it causes, so it cannot rank policies that differ mainly in how much queueing
they cause.

\paragraph{What this settles and what it does not.} Three things change. The chapter's central claim ---
that joint rate-and-split control beats every hand-written alternative --- survives on the packet-level
body after all, provided the policy is trained there; what \cref{sec:p2eval:ns3} refuted was zero-shot
transfer, not the claim. \Cref{sec:p2eval:seeds}'s rule gains its constructive arm: when a surrogate
cannot grade, the remedy is to move the training body, not to sample more surrogate seeds, and here that
move also collapsed the seed spread sixfold. And the mock's standing is now measured rather than
assumed --- it is a cheap training environment that costs $0.228$ QoE and a sixfold spread across seeds,
which is a real price and not a technicality.

Four limits bound it. Three seeds is this chapter's floor, not a comfortable margin, and the arm is
graded over three evaluation seeds of five episodes each. The arm is unconditioned: it is the dynamic
scenario at the nominal intent, so every conditioning, transfer, and mid-call result in this chapter
remains mock-only, and nothing here says an NS-3-native \emph{conditioned} policy would behave the same.
The out-of-distribution finding of \cref{sec:p2eval:regimes} is untouched --- these seeds trained and
were graded on the same distribution. And the comparison is native-trained against mock-trained
\emph{on NS-3}; the reverse penalty is real and symmetric, since the native checkpoints score $0.518$ on
the mock where mock-trained ones score $0.617$. Each backend flatters its own.

\paragraph{What it costs.} The earlier estimate of this cost was wrong by a factor of six. The
${\approx}$\,4\,h per 100-episode seed extrapolated from a 5-episode probe (441\,s against the mock's
436\,s) does not survive contact with a full run, because per-episode cost grows with replay-buffer
occupancy. Measured end to end on the three seeds, they took 24.6\,h, 25.0\,h, and 20.1\,h --- about
\SI{14}{\minute} per episode. Runs are also strictly sequential, because both
\texttt{ns3ai\_utils.Experiment} and \texttt{ns3-ai-msg-interface.h} default the shared-memory segment
name to \texttt{"My Seg"} and nothing overrides it, so two concurrent runs deadlock. The three-seed NS-3
arm therefore cost \textbf{${\approx}$\,70\,h} wall-clock against ${\approx}$\,4.5\,h for the mock arm
run three-up, a factor of roughly 15; grading it cost a further \SI{18}{\minute} for the nine NS-3 cells
and \SI{37}{\minute} for the paired comparison, against \SI{2}{\minute} for the same nine cells on the
mock. That ratio, not a fidelity judgment, is why every \emph{other} policy in this chapter is
mock-trained --- and \cref{tab:p2ns3native} is the measurement of what that convenience costs.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:p2eval:ablation}

The architectural ablation is reported in \cref{sec:p2eval:head}, because its result organizes the
chapter rather than qualifying it. This subsection collects the remaining design choices, including the
three that did not pay off.

\paragraph{Which half of the hierarchy carries the result?} Both, and in a way that depends on the
intent --- on the mock. Removing the transport agent (app-only) costs 65\% of the QoE under
\texttt{interactive} and 16\% under \texttt{passive}; removing the application agent (path-only) costs
10\% and 16\% respectively. Neither half is sufficient, and the difference is what the joint premise of
\cref{sec:p2design:motivation} buys.

Transferred zero-shot to the packet-level body the decomposition does not hold, and this is the sharpest
instance of the sim-to-sim gap of \cref{sec:p2eval:ns3}. There the app-only ablation recovers 80\% of
the full system rather than 35\%, and the path-only ablation does not cost anything at all --- it
\emph{outscores} the full pair, 0.380 against 0.348, within noise.

That inversion, however, is an artifact of transfer rather than of the backend. On the NS-3-native arm
of \cref{sec:p2eval:ns3native} both halves cost something again, and cost more than they did on the
mock: removing the transport agent leaves 69\% of the full system and removing the application agent
leaves 80\%, with the full pair ahead of both by margins of $0.179$ and $0.114$ against a training-seed
spread of $0.019$. The correct statement is therefore narrower than ``the decomposition is
mock-specific'' and more useful: the joint premise holds wherever the policy was trained, and a policy
transferred across backends degrades unevenly, its bitrate controller more than its scheduler.

\paragraph{Domain randomization does not help here, on either axis.} Randomizing the dynamics hazard
rates per training episode costs 0.027 QoE on the nominal environment and loses in all nine evaluation
cells, against training-seed spreads of 0.024 and 0.013. That measures randomization's \emph{cost}; its
intended \emph{benefit} is generalization to networks outside the training distribution, which requires
grading both arms on held-out environments the randomization was supposed to prepare for. Doing so costs
a further 0.033 QoE, again in 9 of 9 cells. The treatment is negative on the axis it was adopted for as
well as on the one it was previously measured on. The interpretation is mechanical: the observation
carries no signal about \emph{which} dynamics draw is in force, so widening the training distribution
adds variance the policy cannot condition on. Consistent with this, the one place randomization clearly
helps is the app-only fallback (0.299 against 0.155), which is what one expects from a treatment that
flattens a policy rather than sharpening it. One held-out environment is uninformative rather than
supportive: on the harsh draw both arms score negative, the ranking is unstable, and the randomized
arm's training-seed spread is 0.445 --- larger than any effect in this chapter.

\paragraph{An attention-pool critic does not help either, and destabilizes training.} Replacing the
masked mean-pool of \cref{sec:p2design:scoring} with a Set-Transformer attention pool --- targeted at
the shared-bottleneck structure the mean-pool is blind to --- is not significant in the mean (0.083 in
the mean pool's favor, inside its confidence interval, won in four of nine cells). The decisive number
is the spread: the attention arm's is 0.151, twelve times the mean-pool's 0.013 and larger than the
entire margin the system claims over any baseline, because one of its three training seeds collapsed
outright. The mean pool remains the default. The reason to report this rather than drop it is that
evaluated on its healthy seed alone the attention pool looked like a small win --- a concrete instance
of the methodological point of \cref{sec:p2eval:setup}, caught in our own data.

\paragraph{DroQ and prioritized replay buy nothing at convergence, and little before it.} The critic
LayerNorm with a raised update-to-data ratio, and prioritized experience replay, are enabled in every
configuration reported in this chapter. Turning all three off costs $-0.005 \pm 0.010$ QoE --- not
significant, positive in two of nine cells --- and both arms rank the learned pair first in every cell,
so no result in this chapter depends on them. Since these are \emph{sample-efficiency} mechanisms and
that comparison is at convergence, the stored training curves test the claim they actually make: the
flags are ahead by $+0.138$ mean training reward over the first ten episodes and reach an early
threshold one to three episodes sooner, but the advantage is gone by episode 25. A real but transient
head start, not a sample-efficiency win --- and a weak version of the claim at that, since the shipped
update-to-data ratio is 2 rather than the 10--20 the DroQ recipe is about, so what is refuted is DroQ
as configured here and not DroQ as published.

\subsection{What the Retractions Have in Common}
\label{sec:p2eval:seeds}

Five results in this chapter looked clean at one sampling of the training seed and did not survive a
larger one. Four broke between one seed and three. The static scenario's reward comparison was stable
and became unstable
(\cref{sec:p2eval:regimes}). Intent specialization held in two of three columns and became detectable in
none (\cref{sec:p2eval:transfer}). The learned pair ranked first on the packet-level backend and became
one of two clusters (\cref{sec:p2eval:ns3}). Global-only conditioning was predicted to be inert and
turned out to be a working policy (\cref{sec:p2eval:conditioning}). We report the pattern rather than
four separate corrections, because the pattern is the more useful finding and it is the one result in
this chapter that generalizes beyond multipath scheduling.

Two of the four were not merely optimistic --- they were the most favorable cell available, without
anyone choosing it. Neither was cherry-picked; both are what one gets by running a configuration,
looking at the number, and writing it down.

The mechanism is a confusion between two spreads that measure different things and that differ by an
order of magnitude on this problem. Confidence intervals over \emph{evaluation} seeds are routinely
$\pm 0.001$--$0.01$ here; the spread over \emph{training} seeds ranges 0.012--0.151 across the arms in
this chapter. A margin reported against the first and not the second looks significant at roughly ten
times its true resolution, and every claim above that broke, broke inside that gap. The recommendation
follows directly and is the methodological position of \cref{sec:p2eval:setup} stated as a result: a
policy is a draw from a learner, the learner is the thing under test, so report the spread over training
runs and not only over evaluation episodes.

A fifth result belongs with the four, and it is a different failure. \Cref{sec:p2eval:conditioning}'s
interpolation tie-breaker looked clean at three training seeds, survived being run at six on its
\emph{pooled} statistic, and fell apart when that statistic was decomposed per training seed: two of six
global-only policies trend in the opposite direction, which a Spearman $\rho$ of 0.800 computed over the
pooled sweep conceals entirely. Nothing was under-sampled and nothing was backend-specific. The summary
statistic was simply computed over the wrong axis first, and pooling across seeds before checking sign
consistency turns ``four runs agree and two disagree'' into one confident-looking number. The rule that
catches it is the same one this section already applies to QoE --- report the per-seed values, not only
their aggregate --- which is why the sweep records them.

Two corollaries are less comfortable and belong with it. First, more seeds buy resolution, not
reliability: doubling from three to six training seeds improved the estimates and did not reduce the
spread (\cref{sec:p2eval:conditioning}). On this problem the learner is genuinely inconsistent run to
run, and \cref{sec:p2eval:ns3} shows what that costs where it matters most. The one intervention that
did shrink the spread was not statistical but mechanical --- closing the observation-normalizer channel
of \cref{tab:p2fixednorm} moved it from 0.041 to 0.024 and from 0.054 to 0.010 --- which is consistent
with the reading above: the spread is a property of the learner and its inputs, and it moves when one of
those changes, not when the estimate of it improves. Second, and sharper, the
sampling remedy does not always exist. The mock--NS-3 decorrelation of \cref{tab:p2seeds} is not
insufficient sampling: over six seeds the mock's own spread is 0.008 and its rank correlation with the
packet-level body is $-0.086$, so \emph{no} amount of mock evaluation identifies which checkpoint
transfers. The lesson upgrades accordingly --- seeds must be sampled \emph{and} graded on the backend
the claim is about.

Both corollaries have a constructive counterpart, and \cref{sec:p2eval:ns3native} supplies it: what did
work was changing the backend rather than the sample size. Training on the packet-level body collapsed
the spread that no amount of mock evaluation could see through, from 0.081 to 0.019, and turned a
three-of-six split into first place in nine of nine cells. This is the same reading as the normalizer
intervention above, one layer out: the spread is a property of the learner \emph{and its environment},
and it moves when one of those changes. So the rule has two arms rather than one. Sample training seeds
and grade them where the claim lives; and when the surrogate cannot grade, the fix is to move the
training body, not to buy more surrogate seeds.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:p2eval:summary}

Seven claims survive this evaluation, and four do not.

The learned pair beats every one of eight comparison points under all three application intents on the
analytical backend, with stable rankings and margins of $+0.065$, $+0.118$, and $+0.143$ QoE over the
strongest hand-written policy, a WebRTC-style deployed stack, against training-seed spreads of
0.018--0.023. The permutation-equivariant path head is what makes that possible: substituting a flat one
costs 0.231 QoE and drops the learned pair from first place in nine of nine cells to none. That head
also delivers what it was adopted for --- one policy, six path counts from two to twelve, no retraining
and no padding, first in nine of nine cells at every count from four up, at a parameter count that does
not move between two paths and ninety-six --- where the flat head cannot be loaded at any path count but
its own. A single policy conditioned on the application's intent matches the best specialist under all
three intents, at a third of the training cost, and adapts continuously across the space between them;
the shortfall the per-path conditioned arm carried is attributable to the observation normalizer rather
than to the conditioning scheme, and closing that channel recovers it. That policy is also the only one
of the two designs that survives the intent \emph{changing during a call}: when the deadline tightens
mid-connection, a specialist that cannot see the change overruns its new budget six times over for ten
seconds, where the conditioned policy never crosses it. The scheduler's contribution
scales with the heterogeneity of path headroom, with the number of paths it must divide a fixed capacity
among, and with the strictness of the application's intent --- the last being the axis this chapter
shares with \cref{ch:single_path} --- and the first and third of those interact rather than adding. And
\cref{prop:p2cancel} holds exactly for additively separable scorers, discharging for that function class
the claim that the cancellation of \cref{ch:single_path} covers softmax as well as argmax selectors.
The seventh claim is the one this evaluation added last and the one that most changes the reading of the
rest: trained on the packet-level body rather than transferred to it, the learned pair leads the same
comparison set by $0.114$ QoE at a training-seed spread of $0.019$ and ranks first in nine of nine
cells, beating mock-trained transfer on identical cells by $0.228$ with every cell positive.

Four claims do not survive, and one more is bounded rather than refuted: at six training seeds per arm
this design resolves QoE differences no smaller than \SIrange{0.063}{0.087}{}, which is larger than
every specialist margin it is asked about, so the conditioning parity result bounds the difference
rather than demonstrating equivalence. Of the four that fail outright, the first is that intents do not
produce measurably intent-specific policies: no margin in
the transfer matrix clears its own training-seed spread. The ranking does not transfer zero-shot to the
packet-level backend: three of six checkpoints keep first place and three do not, and mock score does
not predict which --- a failure of transfer rather than of the method, since training on that backend
restores first place in every cell, but the zero-shot claim as originally made is gone. Global-only
conditioning is not inert, as \cref{prop:p2cancel} would suggest for
a system whose scorer met its premise: a trained nonlinear encoder routes the intent past the
cancellation, so the proposition constrains the architecture for exactly one layer. And the headline
claim is bounded by the distribution it was trained on rather than general: it holds on the nominal
network and on a milder held-out one, and on a harsher held-out one the learned pair ranks first in zero
of nine cells at two of three intents and scores below plain GCC. Invariance to the path count does not
buy invariance to the capacity behind those paths.

The negatives are the more valuable half. Together they say that the theory identifies where
conditioning must enter for a \emph{guarantee} while the experiment shows a deep encoder arriving there
without one; that a cheap surrogate is useless for selecting among the policies it produces, and, once
the comparison is run, is not a free training environment either but one that costs $0.228$ QoE and a
sixfold seed spread; and that a learned scheduler's advantage over hand-written rules is a function of
the regime it is measured in, in both directions. All three are stronger statements than the results
they replaced.
