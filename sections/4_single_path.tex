\chapter{Motivating Study: Intent-Conditioned Single-Path Selection}
\label{ch:single_path}

This chapter investigates the first and most basic control decision a SCION endpoint faces: given a
running application's intent and a changing network, \emph{which} of the candidate paths to a destination
should carry a flow? We answer it in five steps. \Cref{sec:sp:framework} builds the selection framework --
the simulation environment, the decision problem, and a permutation-equivariant scoring architecture that
copes with a variable path set. \Cref{sec:p1design:conditioning} adds the chapter's central contribution:
we show that \emph{where} an intent signal enters a comparison-based policy decides whether it can change
the chosen path at all, and condition the per-path features accordingly. \Cref{sec:sp:impl} describes the
implementation, the baselines, and the per-context oracle against which everything is measured.
\Cref{sec:sp:eval} evaluates the conditioned selector on held-out hours, on intents it never trained on,
and on candidate sets whose size it never saw. Finally, \cref{sec:p1eval:ceiling} identifies the
single-path performance ceiling that motivates the multipath system of \cref{ch:multipath}.

\section{Selection Framework: Environment, Decision, and Scoring}
\label{sec:sp:framework}

We start from the DQN path selector of Bourak~et~al.~\cite{Bourak2025} and identify three limitations we
set out to remove (\cref{sec:p1design:strawman}). We then describe the improved simulation environment
(\cref{sec:p1design:env}), the state, action, and reward that define the decision problem
(\cref{sec:p1design:mdp}), and the permutation-equivariant path-scoring architecture that handles a
variable path set (\cref{sec:p1design:scoring}).

\subsection{Starting Point and Its Limitations}
\label{sec:p1design:strawman}

Bourak~et~al.~\cite{Bourak2025} frame SCION path selection as a reinforcement-learning problem and
train a DQN to pick a path from per-path measurements of latency, loss, and bandwidth, rewarding a
weighted combination of goodput and trust while charging for probing. They report oracle-level
selection quality at a fraction of the probing cost of measuring every path. This validates the RL
framing and gives us a reward structure to build on, but three design choices limit it.

\begin{description}
  \item[Fixed action space.] A vanilla DQN has one output per action, so the number of paths must be
  fixed and known. Real source--destination pairs have different, time-varying path counts; padding to
  a maximum wastes capacity and still caps the topology size.
  \item[Single baked-in objective.] The reward weights are constants chosen before training. The
  resulting policy embodies \emph{one} trade-off between goodput and trust; a different application
  intent demands a different, separately trained model.
  \item[Environment fidelity.] Capturing when path selection actually matters requires paths that
  genuinely share bottlenecks and conditions that vary over realistic timescales, so that a good choice
  at one hour is a poor choice at another.
\end{description}

Our design removes all three: a scoring architecture for the first, per-path intent conditioning for the
second, and an improved environment for the third. A strawman for the second limitation -- appending the
reward weights to the state of the existing DQN -- is instructive precisely because of \emph{where} it
fails, as we show in \cref{sec:p1design:conditioning}.

The third limitation deserves a number rather than an assertion, since it bounds the size of every effect
this chapter reports. The quantity that settles it is the spread of achievable goodput across a pair's
candidate paths \emph{within a single decision context} -- exactly what a selector has to exploit. We
measure it in \cref{sec:p1design:env} and find it wide and time-varying, so path choice is a first-order
effect in this environment.

\paragraph{What our fixed-action control is, and what it is not.} The lowest rung of the agent ladder in
\cref{sec:p1impl:agents} --- the \emph{Flat DQN} --- is the foil against which the architectural results
of \cref{sec:p1eval:ablation} are measured, so we fix its status here rather than let the comparison
imply more than it supports. It is \textbf{a fixed-action-space control of our own construction, not a
reimplementation of Bourak~et~al.} It observes a five-dimensional aggregate context --- day, hour, mean
utilization across the pair's candidate paths, mean trust, and the fraction of those paths above a load
threshold --- and selects by index into a padded 30-way action head. Three consequences follow, all of
them properties of our control rather than of the published system: the per-path metrics are reduced to
means before it sees them, so it has no per-path features at all; available bandwidth, the quantity that
decides goodput, never enters its observation, since mean utilization is a load \emph{ratio} carrying no
absolute capacity; and its observation omits the source--destination pair, so it cannot condition on
which pair it is serving even though its action indexes that pair's path list.

The reward and goodput gaps we report between this control and the scoring agents therefore confound
three differences at once --- the fixed action space, which is the variable we mean to test, aggregate
rather than per-path observations, and the missing pair identity --- and we read them as system-level
comparisons between two designs rather than as an isolation of the action space. What does isolate the
architecture is the evidence that holds whatever features an agent is given: an action that is an index
into a padded vector cannot be invariant to the order of the candidates, and cannot address a set whose
size it was not compiled for. \Cref{sec:p1eval:dynamic} measures both directly, and neither depends on
what the control observes.

\subsection{Improved SCION Simulation Environment}
\label{sec:p1design:env}

Training and evaluating a path selector requires an environment in which the ``right'' path changes
with conditions. We construct one in several stages.

\paragraph{Topology.} AS-level topologies are generated with BRITE and converted into SCION structures
-- ISDs, core and non-core ASes, and inter/intra-ISD links -- so that the graph has realistic degree
and locality structure rather than being hand-drawn. We describe the conversion concretely, because the
capacity heterogeneity the agent exploits comes from the converter rather than from BRITE, and a reader
who assumes BRITE-generated bandwidths will misread the results. BRITE runs its AS-level
Barab\'{a}si--Albert model on 90~nodes with the bandwidth distribution pinned to a constant, so
\emph{every one of its 382~links has exactly \SI{10}{\giga\bit\per\second} of capacity}. The converter
assigns ISDs by $k$-means on node coordinates and core ASes by degree rank, then adds 146~links of its
own with hard-coded capacities (\cref{tab:p1design:capacities}); these additions are the sole source of
capacity variation in the graph. Link latency is likewise not BRITE's delay column, which is on an
abstract scale, but $\max(0.5,\,0.02\,d)$ milliseconds in the node coordinate plane, giving a median
inter-AS latency of \SI{8.5}{\milli\second} and a maximum of \SI{23.0}{\milli\second}.

\paragraph{Control plane.} A beaconing simulation propagates path-segment construction beacons
top-down within each ISD and across the core mesh between ISDs, yielding the up/core/down segments that
compose into end-to-end paths. Peer links are excluded. The result is, for each source--destination
pair, a realistic set of candidate paths of varying length and quality -- the agent's action set.

\paragraph{Traffic and link dynamics.} We drive the network with 28~days of hourly demand: foreground
flows for every routable pair plus randomized background traffic, modulated by diurnal and weekly
factors. Crucially, load is aggregated \emph{per link}, so paths that share a bottleneck link contend
for its capacity. This is what makes selection non-trivial: the utilization and effective bandwidth of
a path depend on what every other flow is doing at that hour, and the best path at 3\,a.m. is often
congested at 8\,p.m.

\paragraph{Link and path model.} A link's utilization is its aggregate offered load over its capacity,
capped at 1.5; its latency is a base propagation delay scaled by a queueing multiplier that grows with
utilization and saturates at $2\times$; and its loss rate is a piecewise function of utilization alone,
zero below 0.8 and capped at 0.2. A path's latency is the sum over its links, its loss the complement of
the product of per-link survival probabilities, its utilization the maximum over its links, and its
\emph{available bandwidth} the minimum residual capacity $\max(0,\,C_e - \mathrm{load}_e)$ across its
links.

Two consequences of this model bound how the chapter's results should be read. First, the model is
\emph{open-loop}: the agent's own selection
never adds load to the links it traverses, and there is no per-flow fair share or congestion feedback.
This changes what the chapter's central quantity means, so we give it a definition of its own below.
Second, utilization is capped at 1.5 rather than 1.0, so links can be
oversubscribed; this is what produces the $>1.0$ abscissa values in \cref{fig:p1eval:ceiling}. Both are
defensible choices for a study of \emph{selection} -- which path is best is well defined without
modeling the flow's own back-pressure -- but neither would survive into a closed-loop transport study,
and \cref{ch:multipath} accordingly uses a different data plane.

\paragraph{A standing note on the word ``goodput''.} Every absolute rate this chapter quotes inherits the
open-loop assumption, so the quantity needs a definition and not merely a caveat. Throughout
\cref{ch:single_path}, \emph{goodput} denotes the \textbf{residual bottleneck capacity} of the chosen
path --- the minimum spare capacity across its links, measured while this flow contributes no load of its
own --- and never a rate an application achieved. It is the right quantity for grading a
\emph{selection}, since it is exactly what distinguishes a good candidate from a bad one at a given hour,
and it is the quantity the reward of \cref{eq:p1reward} normalizes. It is not a throughput measurement,
and no number in this chapter should be compared against one. We keep the word because it names the
reward term and labels the figure axes. \Cref{ch:multipath}, by contrast, measures rates that flows
actually deliver over a packet-level data plane, so its throughput figures and this chapter's capacity
figures are different quantities --- a distinction that matters most at \cref{sec:p1eval:ceiling}, whose
bridging argument concerns capacity a single path cannot supply.

\paragraph{Does path choice matter here?} The environment is only interesting if the candidate paths of
a pair differ materially at a given hour, so we measure that spread directly over all 10752~held-out
decision contexts (\cref{sec:p1eval:setup}). Within a single context, the best candidate carries
\textbf{3.04$\times$} the median candidate's goodput (p10 1.57$\times$, p90 18.26$\times$) and
\SI{7983}{\mega\bit\per\second} more than the worst; the coefficient of variation of goodput across
candidates has median 1.43, and the worst candidate's latency exceeds the best's by
\SI{29.3}{\milli\second} at the median, a factor of 3.5. The choice is also genuinely time-varying: the
\emph{identity} of the highest-goodput path changes between consecutive hours in 8.6\% of cases, so a
policy cannot memorize one answer per pair. At the network level, p90 link utilization is 0.76 with
7.8\% of links oversubscribed, and only 0.09\% of contexts have no usable bandwidth on any candidate.
Path choice is therefore a first-order effect in this environment, which is the premise
\cref{sec:p1design:strawman} needs.

\paragraph{Probing model.} Fresh metrics are not free. The environment charges a latency probe
\SI{10}{\milli\second} plus \SI{0.5}{\milli\second} per hop, and a full bandwidth probe
\SI{100}{\milli\second} plus \SI{20}{\milli\second} per hop; it credits the agent with having probed
only the path(s) it actually inspects. Because both costs grow with hop count, probing is cheaper on
short paths -- a fact \cref{sec:p1eval:overhead} returns to. The reward charges the \emph{mean} cost per
probe issued in a step, normalized by a \SI{500}{\milli\second} reference. This turns ``measure less''
into a real objective rather than an afterthought, and lets us compare the learned selector's probing
footprint against baselines that probe exhaustively.

Charging the \emph{mean} rather than the total cost is consequential and deserves justification, because
it means a method issuing thirty cheap probes is penalized less by the reward than one issuing a single
expensive probe. This is deliberate: the per-probe charge measures cost per unit of information gained,
which keeps the reward a statement about \emph{selection quality} and prevents the probing term from
dominating the goodput and trust terms it is meant to modulate. The consequence is that the reward
understates the probing gap -- under the Balanced intent the learned selector is charged
\SI{136}{\milli\second} per probe against exhaustive widest-path selection's
\SI{180}{\milli\second}, a near-tie, while their \emph{total} measurement cost differs by a factor
of~40. We therefore report total probing cost separately in \cref{sec:p1eval:overhead} rather than
relying on the reward to express it.

\subsection{Decision Problem: State, Action, Reward}
\label{sec:p1design:mdp}

\paragraph{State.} The agent observes a \emph{global} context and a \emph{per-path} feature matrix. The
global context summarizes time and aggregate conditions -- normalized day-of-week and hour-of-day, mean
path utilization, mean trust, and the fraction of paths whose utilization exceeds 0.7 -- plus a compact
encoding of the source and destination. The three aggregate terms are computed over the current flow's
own candidate path set, not over the whole network. Each candidate path contributes a seven-dimensional
feature row: latency normalized by \SI{100}{\milli\second}, loss rate, hop count over 20, the path's
bandwidth relative to the best candidate at that hour, utilization, a static-bandwidth term, and a trust
score. Because the number of rows $N$ varies, the state is naturally a (global, path-matrix) pair rather
than a flat vector.

\paragraph{Action.} The action is the choice of one path, i.e.\ an index into the current candidate set.
Because $N$ varies, the action is realized as an $\arg\max$ over per-path scores (\cref{sec:p1design:scoring})
with invalid entries masked, rather than as a fixed categorical head.

\paragraph{Reward.} We adopt and generalize the goodput--trust reward of
Bourak~et~al.~\cite{Bourak2025}. For the chosen path, let $\mathrm{goodput}\in[0,1]$ be the achieved
bandwidth normalized by the \emph{best available bandwidth among that pair's candidate paths at that
hour}, and define a trust term from loss and delay,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{trust} = \max\!\big(0,\; 1 - (w_3\,\mathrm{loss} + w_4\,\mathrm{delay})\big),
\label{eq:p1trust}
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{delay}$ the latency capped at \SI{100}{\milli\second} and normalized. As
\cref{sec:background:scion} states, this quantity is a \emph{performance proxy} inherited from the
reward of Bourak~et~al.\ and not trust in the SCION control-plane sense: it is computed from measured
loss and delay alone and carries no AS reputation or path-authentication content. The base reward
trades goodput against trust and is shifted to $[-1,1]$, and a probing penalty is subtracted:
\begin{equation}
r = \underbrace{2\big(w_1\,\mathrm{goodput} + w_2\,\mathrm{trust}\big) - 1}_{\text{base reward}}
    \;-\; w_{\text{probe}}\cdot \mathrm{probe\_penalty},
\label{eq:p1reward}
\end{equation}
clamped to $[-1,1]$, where $\mathrm{probe\_penalty}$ is the probing cost normalized to $[0,1]$. We take
$w_1 + w_2 = 1$ throughout, which is what places the base reward in $[-1,1]$ before clamping. The
five weights $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, w_2, w_3, w_4, w_{\text{probe}})$ are exactly the \emph{application
intent}: default balanced values are $(0.7, 0.3, 0.5, 0.5, 0.05)$, but a throughput-hungry intent
pushes $w_1$ toward~1, a loss-averse intent raises $w_2$ and $w_3$, a latency-sensitive intent raises
$w_4$, and a probe-frugal intent raises $w_{\text{probe}}$. The design goal of
\cref{sec:p1design:conditioning} is a \emph{single} policy that behaves correctly for \emph{any} setting
of $\mathbf{w}$.

Two properties of this reward shape every result in the chapter, and we state them explicitly because
both are easy to misread. First, goodput is normalized against the \emph{best candidate path of that
pair at that hour}, so it is a relative score rather than an absolute rate: a policy that always picks
the best available path scores near~1 whether that path carries \SI{9}{\giga\bit\per\second} or
\SI{2}{\giga\bit\per\second}. The reward therefore measures selection quality and is blind by
construction to how much capacity exists to select from -- which is why, in \cref{sec:p1eval:ceiling},
the learned selector's reward stays flat across the congestion range while its goodput falls by a
quarter. Second, latency enters the objective only \emph{inside} the trust term, through $w_4$; there is
no standalone latency weight. A latency-sensitive intent is expressed by raising $w_2$ and $w_4$
together, which is how the \emph{Low-Latency} profile of \cref{tab:app:profiles} is defined.

\subsection{Handling a Variable Path Set: The Scoring Architecture}
\label{sec:p1design:scoring}

To make the policy independent of the path count, we score each path with a \emph{shared} network and
select by comparison, following the permutation-equivariant design of \cref{sec:background:variable}.
The global context is broadcast and concatenated to every path's feature row, and a shared multilayer
perceptron maps each $(\text{global}, \text{path}_i)$ pair to a scalar $Q$-value. Selection is
$\arg\max_i Q_i$; absent or invalid paths are masked to $-10^9$ so they can never be chosen and do not
distort training targets.

We use a \emph{dueling} scoring head (\cref{sec:background:rl}): a value stream reads the global context
alone to produce $V(s)$, an advantage stream reads the per-path features to produce $A(s, i)$, and the
two combine as $Q_i = V(s) + \big(A(s,i) - \operatorname{mean}_{j\in\text{valid}} A(s,j)\big)$ with the
mean taken only over valid paths. Because many paths are often comparably good, separating ``how good is
this situation'' from ``which path is marginally better'' stabilizes learning. Double DQN targets and
prioritized replay (\cref{sec:background:rl}) further stabilize and accelerate training. The scoring
design also delivers the dynamic-action property directly: the same weights serve a pair with three
paths and a pair with thirty, and are invariant to how the paths happen to be ordered.

\section{Where Intent Conditioning Must Enter}
\label{sec:p1design:conditioning}

The central contribution of this chapter is making one scoring policy serve every intent $\mathbf{w}$.
The finding is that this is not a matter of feeding the policy its intent: \emph{where} the intent enters
decides whether it can influence the choice at all.

\subsection{Where conditioning cancels}
\label{sec:p1design:concat}
The obvious approach is to append $\mathbf{w}$ to the state. Whether that works depends entirely on
\emph{which} part of the state it is appended to, and the reason is a one-line argument about the
comparison that defines the action.

\begin{proposition}[Common-mode cancellation]
\label{prop:p1cancel}
Let a scoring policy assign path~$i$ the score
$Q_i(s, \mathbf{w}) = f(s, f_i) + c(s, \mathbf{w})$, where $f_i$ is path~$i$'s feature row and the
conditioning enters only through the term $c$, which does not depend on~$i$. Then
$\arg\max_i Q_i(s,\mathbf{w})$ is independent of $\mathbf{w}$: the policy consumes the intent but its
action is invariant to it.
\end{proposition}

\noindent The proof is immediate --- $c(s,\mathbf{w})$ is a constant offset across the compared
alternatives, so it cancels in every pairwise comparison $Q_i - Q_j$ and cannot change which index
attains the maximum. The point is that this is not a statement about optimization difficulty or sample
efficiency: no amount of training can overcome it, because the intent-dependent term is not in the image
of the decision rule at all.

The dueling scoring head of \cref{sec:p1design:scoring} instantiates exactly this hypothesis when the
intent is routed into the value stream. There $Q_i = V(s, \mathbf{w}) + \big(A(s,i) -
\operatorname{mean}_j A(s,j)\big)$, so $V$ plays the role of $c$: conditioning the value stream is
provably inert. We verify this directly at the network level -- holding path features fixed and varying
only $\mathbf{w}$ leaves every pairwise $Q$-difference constant to within $10^{-5}$ -- and
\cref{sec:p1eval:ablation} confirms the behavioral consequence over five training seeds.

One qualification matters, because it is the difference between ``cannot re-rank'' and ``re-ranks only
through a channel it does not control''. \Cref{prop:p1cancel} is a statement about the
\emph{architecture}, and in the deployed system its premise is mildly violated: the seventh per-path
feature is the trust score, which is itself computed from $w_3$ and $w_4$. Intent therefore does leak
into $f_i$ through one feature, and this is precisely why the value-stream variant of
\cref{sec:p1eval:ablation} attains a small but nonzero behavioral divergence rather than exactly zero.
The measured residual is what the leak predicts, which sharpens the proposition rather than weakening
it.

\subsection{Conditioning the per-path comparison}
\label{sec:p1design:perpath}
The fix follows directly from \cref{prop:p1cancel}: the intent must reach the term that distinguishes
one candidate from another. The scoring architecture of \cref{sec:p1design:scoring} already broadcasts
the global context to every path row before the advantage stream, so it suffices to make the intent part
of that context. Writing $\tilde{s} = [\,s \,\|\, \mathbf{w}\,]$ for the widened global vector, with
$[\,\cdot\,\|\,\cdot\,]$ denoting concatenation, the scores become
\begin{equation}
Q_i = V(\tilde{s}) + \Big(A\big([\,\tilde{s} \,\|\, f_i\,]\big)
      - \operatorname{mean}_{j \in \text{valid}} A\big([\,\tilde{s} \,\|\, f_j\,]\big)\Big).
\label{eq:p1cond}
\end{equation}
The advantage stream now evaluates $\mathbf{w}$ jointly with each path's own feature row, and because the
shared scorer is nonlinear, the same $\mathbf{w}$ shifts two candidates by different amounts, so the
ordering can change. This is not of the form \cref{prop:p1cancel} assumes and the cancellation argument
does not apply. The intent also reaches the value stream, which is why we call the agent
\emph{Two-Stream-Concat}; by the proposition that half is inert for the choice, and it is the advantage
stream that does the work.

We adopt this mechanism because it is the least the proposition permits. It introduces no conditioning
sub-network, no new hyperparameters, and no new architectural component: it widens the existing global
context by the five intent weights and lets the existing broadcast do the rest, for
$5 \times 128 \times 2 = 1280$ additional parameters -- one input weight per hidden unit of each stream.
It also inherits the architecture's independence of the path count unchanged, since the intent is
broadcast rather than replicated per candidate. \Cref{sec:p1eval:ablation} shows this captures 87\% of
the re-ranking a per-context oracle achieves, which leaves little room for a more elaborate mechanism to
improve on. The finding this chapter reports is about \emph{where} the intent enters, not about the
machinery that carries it once it is in the right place.

\subsection{Training across intents}
To learn a policy that spans the intent space we train across six \emph{reward profiles} -- named
weight vectors spanning a throughput extreme, a loss-averse extreme, a latency-averse extreme, a balanced
midpoint, and two probe-frugality settings (\cref{tab:app:profiles}). Each training episode is assigned a
profile, sets the environment's reward weights to it, and feeds the same weights to the scorer, so the
agent simultaneously experiences the reward for an intent and the encoding of that intent. Profiles are
scheduled in a stratified fashion -- each profile repeated to fill the episode budget, then shuffled --
so every intent receives an equal number of episodes. The intent is presented to the network in a
rescaled encoding rather than as raw weights, which keeps the conditioning inputs on a comparable scale
to the path features.

Training on a finite set of profiles is a means, not the goal. What we want is a policy that maps the
\emph{intent space} to behavior, so that an unseen $\mathbf{w}$ presented at inference -- in particular
one interpolating between two trained profiles -- yields correspondingly interpolated behavior without
retraining. This is the zero-shot generalization property required by \cref{sec:problem:properties}, and
\cref{sec:p1eval:zeroshot} tests it directly.

\subsection{Relation to Mortise}
This is the path-selection instance of Mortise's principle~\cite{Shen2026}. Where Mortise maps an
application's revealed QoS preference to congestion-control \emph{parameters}, our scorer makes an
explicit intent vector an input to the per-path comparison that determines which path is chosen. In
both, intent is a first-class input that reshapes a network mechanism, not a constant compiled into it.
\Cref{ch:multipath} pushes the same principle one step further, letting intent shape not only the network's
choice but the application's own output.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:sp:impl}

This section describes how the design is realized: the agent family and their architectures
(\cref{sec:p1impl:agents}), the end-to-end evaluation pipeline that turns a topology into trained models
and comparable results (\cref{sec:p1impl:pipeline}), and the baselines and probing accounting against
which the learned selectors are measured (\cref{sec:p1impl:baselines}). The implementation is in Python
(PyTorch for the agents) and is organized so that each pipeline stage passes data to the next through
timestamped run directories.

\subsection{Agent Family}
\label{sec:p1impl:agents}

The codebase implements a family of agents of increasing capability, from which the ablation ladder of
\cref{sec:p1eval:ablation} is drawn.

\begin{description}
  \item[Flat DQN (simple / enhanced).] A conventional fixed-action DQN over the 5-dimensional aggregate
  state of \cref{sec:p1design:strawman}, selecting by index into a padded 30-way action head. The
  \emph{simple} variant is a small multilayer perceptron with $\varepsilon$-greedy
  exploration and a periodic target network; the \emph{enhanced} variant adds Double
  DQN~\cite{VanHasselt2016}, a dueling head~\cite{Wang2016}, prioritized experience
  replay~\cite{Schaul2016}, soft target updates, and action masking. These agents cannot handle a
  variable path set. It is a control of our own construction rather than a reimplementation of
  Bourak~et~al.~\cite{Bourak2025}, with the scoping \cref{sec:p1design:strawman} states.
  \item[Path-scoring DQN (simple / enhanced).] The permutation-equivariant design of
  \cref{sec:p1design:scoring}: a shared per-path scorer producing one $Q$-value per path, with variable
  $N$ handled by padding to the batch maximum and masking. The enhanced variant uses the dueling scoring
  head, Double DQN, and prioritized replay. This family delivers the dynamic-action property.
  \item[Conditional path-scoring DQN (\emph{Two-Stream-Concat}).] The recommended agent, adding the
  conditioning of \cref{sec:p1design:perpath}: the five-dimensional encoded intent widens the global
  context, which the scoring head already broadcasts to both streams, so the advantage stream evaluates
  the intent jointly with each candidate's features. Two intent encodings are supported and recorded in
  the checkpoint -- the raw weights, or a policy-friendly rescaling used in all reported runs.
  \item[Value-Concat.] The control that isolates \cref{prop:p1cancel}. It is identical to the above
  except that the intent is routed to the value stream alone, leaving the advantage stream on the
  unwidened context and the raw path features; by the proposition it cannot re-rank.
\end{description}

All four rungs were trained on the same environment, the same 28-day demand trace, and the same
optimizer settings, so the ladder isolates one architectural change at a time: flat to scoring
(per-path features and a variable action set), scoring to Value-Concat (intent present but
path-independent), and Value-Concat to Two-Stream-Concat (intent reaching the per-path comparison).

\paragraph{Training details.} All agents use two hidden layers of width 128, learning rate $10^{-3}$,
discount $\gamma = 0.95$, batch size 32, a replay buffer of $10^4$ transitions, and soft (Polyak) target
updates with $\tau = 0.05$. Prioritized replay uses priority exponent $\alpha = 0.6$ with the
importance-sampling exponent $\beta$ annealed from $0.4$ to $1.0$. Exploration is $\varepsilon$-greedy,
decayed multiplicatively per episode. Invalid actions are masked to a large negative value both when
acting and when forming bootstrap targets, so masked paths never contribute to the loss. Episodes are
24~steps (one simulated day); the flat agent trains for 533~episodes and the conditional agents for 666,
with one gradient step every four environment steps. The conditional agents are trained across
reward profiles on a stratified schedule (\cref{sec:p1design:conditioning}), so that within a single training run
the network sees every intent an equal number of times.

This budget is short, with two consequences. At one gradient step
per four environment steps, 533 and 666~episodes amount to roughly 3200 and 4000~optimizer updates in
total, and $\varepsilon$ decays only to 0.069 (flat) and 0.036 (conditional), never reaching the stated
0.01 floor. The natural worry is that the weakest rung of the ladder is undertrained rather than
architecturally limited, which would inflate the gap the ablation attributes to the architecture. The
training curves (\cref{app:extra}) settle it: the flat DQN plateaus at a mean episode reward of
$+0.049$ with a training loss that \emph{rises} over the run, while the unconditioned scoring agent,
given the identical 533~episodes on the identical data with the identical optimizer, reaches $+0.809$ --
sixteen times the final reward. The flat agent's deficit is therefore a property of its design --- a fixed
30-way action head over a five-dimensional aggregate state with no per-path features
(\cref{sec:p1design:strawman}) --- and not of its training budget, which is the alternative this
paragraph set out to rule out.
The conditional agents converge to $+0.814$ (Two-Stream-Concat) and $+0.816$ (Value-Concat) over
666~episodes. Every agent in the ladder was retrained under five random seeds, and
\cref{sec:p1eval:ablation} reports the resulting spread.

\subsection{Evaluation Pipeline}
\label{sec:p1impl:pipeline}

The system is driven by a six-stage pipeline, each stage writing artifacts consumed by the next:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Topology generation} -- BRITE produces an AS graph, converted into SCION roles, ISDs, and
  links.
  \item \textbf{Beaconing} -- PCB propagation discovers up/core/down segments and composes candidate paths
  per source--destination pair.
  \item \textbf{Traffic simulation} -- 28~days of hourly demand with per-link aggregation, producing the
  time-varying link states the environment replays.
  \item \textbf{Training} -- all agent variants are trained on the resulting environment; checkpoints and
  per-episode statistics are saved.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation} -- every method (learned and baseline) is run over held-out pairs, hours, and
  intents, logging reward, achieved latency and bandwidth, probing overhead, and selection time.
  \item \textbf{Figures} -- results are rendered in a consistent style for the report.
\end{enumerate}

Running the whole pipeline is a single command; intermediate artifacts (topology, path store, link
states, checkpoints, results tables) live under a timestamped run directory so that any stage can be
re-run or inspected in isolation. Key knobs -- topology size, number of training episodes, the cap on
training pairs, and learning hyperparameters -- are exposed as configuration so that experiments can be
scaled up or down.

\subsection{Baselines and Probing Accounting}
\label{sec:p1impl:baselines}

\paragraph{Baselines.} Six heuristic selectors provide the comparison points. \emph{Shortest-path} takes
the fewest AS hops and \emph{lowest-latency} the smallest measured delay. \emph{Widest-path} takes the
largest measured available bandwidth, and is the only baseline requiring a full bandwidth probe on every
candidate. \emph{ECMP} restricts to the minimum-hop set and pins the flow to one member by hashing its
five-tuple, and the \emph{SCION default} takes the minimum hop count with ties broken by latency. Finally
\emph{random} fixes one pseudo-randomly chosen path per source--destination pair and keeps it for the
whole evaluation, which makes it a static-misconfiguration reference rather than a per-decision coin
flip. Together they span the spectrum from naive to strong single-metric heuristics (widest for
throughput, lowest-latency for delay), so that the learned selectors must beat not just a weak baseline
but the \emph{right} heuristic for each intent.

\paragraph{Oracle.} None of these heuristics is an upper bound. Widest-path maximizes bandwidth, which is
the correct target only under the Throughput intent; under Low-Latency it is actively wrong. We therefore
also evaluate a per-context \emph{oracle}: for each decision it enumerates the candidate set and selects
the path maximizing the \emph{true reward under the intent being scored}. It is charged one full probe,
so its probing cost is comparable to the learned selector's, and its goodput is normalized identically --
by that pair's best candidate at that hour. The oracle is not a deployable policy, since it requires
perfect knowledge of every candidate's realized metrics before choosing; it is the per-intent upper bound
that turns \cref{tab:p1eval:ablation} into a normalized optimality gap and lets us say how much of the
available quality each method captures.

\paragraph{Probing accounting.} A fair comparison must charge each method for the measurements it needs.
The environment therefore records, per decision, which paths a method probed and at what cost -- latency
probes being cheap and full bandwidth probes expensive (\cref{sec:p1design:env}). Heuristics that require
a metric on every path (e.g.\ widest-path) incur the cost of probing all paths, whereas the learned
selector is charged only for the paths it actually inspects. This makes the probing-overhead reduction a
directly measurable quantity rather than an assumed benefit, and is the mechanism by which we test the
high-quality-at-low-probing claim of Bourak~et~al.~\cite{Bourak2025}.

Concretely, and since this drives the whole of \cref{fig:p1eval:probing}: widest-path is charged a full
bandwidth probe on all 30~candidates, \SI{5388}{\milli\second} per selection; every other heuristic a
latency probe on all 30, \SI{360}{\milli\second}; and the learned selector a full bandwidth probe on the
single path it selects, \SIrange{132}{138}{\milli\second} depending on that path's hop count.

One label needs correcting. Our ECMP baseline is evaluated with a constant flow identifier, so it hashes
every flow of a pair onto the same member of the minimum-hop set and exhibits none of the load spreading
its name implies. It is \emph{hash-pinned shortest path} here, and we call it that where the distinction
matters; we retain the ECMP label in tables for comparability with the literature.

\section{Experimental Evaluation and Intent Alignment}
\label{sec:sp:eval}

This section evaluates the selectors on held-out hours. We first fix the
experimental setup and metrics (\cref{sec:p1eval:setup}). We then answer five questions the design
raises, in turn: where must intent enter for conditioning to have any effect
(\cref{sec:p1eval:ablation})? Does the conditioned policy re-rank paths in the \emph{right} direction as
the intent changes (\cref{sec:p1eval:intent})? Does it generalize to intents it never trained on
(\cref{sec:p1eval:zeroshot})? Does the scoring architecture in fact deliver the dynamic-action property
it was chosen for (\cref{sec:p1eval:dynamic})? And does it retain the high-quality-at-low-probing
property of the selector it builds on (\cref{sec:p1eval:overhead})? \Cref{sec:p1eval:ceiling} then turns
to the limit that motivates the next chapter.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:p1eval:setup}

\paragraph{Environment.} Experiments use one BRITE-generated SCION topology of 90~ASes and 528~inter-AS
links, partitioned into 2~ISDs with 6~core ASes. Beaconing (\cref{sec:p1impl:pipeline}) discovers
1004~core segments and 1127~up/down segments, which compose into candidate paths for 8010~routable
source--destination pairs, each with up to 30~paths. The network is driven by the 28-day hourly demand
model with per-link aggregation (\cref{sec:p1design:env}).

\paragraph{Train/test split.} The main tables use a \emph{temporal} split: training uses the first
14~days and every number is measured on the last 14~days (336~held-out hours), a window the agents never
trained on. Unless noted, each cell aggregates 32~pairs across those 336~hours, i.e.\ 10752~independent
decision contexts. We are explicit that this is a temporal split rather than a pair split, because the
32~evaluation pairs are drawn from the same pool the agents train on. Training episodes sample a pair
uniformly from all 8010~routable pairs and visit 644~distinct ones over the run, so only 2 of the
32~evaluation pairs were ever seen; the weakness of this set is not contamination but homogeneity, since
all 32 share the same source AS.

\paragraph{Structurally held-out pairs.} To test generalization across the topology rather than across
time, we additionally construct an exactly disjoint evaluation set: replaying the training RNG identifies
the 644~visited pairs, and we sample 32~pairs from the remaining 7366, chosen to span 32~distinct source
ASes. No agent has seen any of them. \Cref{tab:p1eval:heldout} reports the ablation on this set.

\paragraph{Intents.} The conditional agent is trained across the six reward profiles of
\cref{sec:p1design:conditioning} and evaluated on the four \emph{selection-relevant} ones:
throughput-maximizing (\emph{Throughput}), latency-averse (\emph{Low-Latency}), loss-averse
(\emph{Low-Loss}), and a balanced midpoint (\emph{Balanced}); their exact weight vectors are given in
\cref{tab:app:profiles}. The two probe-frugality profiles are excluded from the conditioning studies
because a single-path selector issues one probe per decision regardless of $w_{\text{probe}}$, so the
weight has little leverage over the choice; probing cost is instead the subject of
\cref{sec:p1eval:overhead}.

This exclusion needs a measured justification rather than an assertion, because probe cost is charged
\emph{per hop} (\cref{sec:p1design:env}) and $w_{\text{probe}}$ is therefore not strictly
path-independent: a probe-frugal intent does have some incentive to prefer short paths. The incentive is
real but negligible. Evaluating all six profiles as intents, the mean chosen hop count of the
\emph{oracle} -- which bounds what any policy could do -- moves from 1.899 under \texttt{probe\_minimal}
($w_{\text{probe}} = 0.001$) to 1.881 under \texttt{probe\_averse} ($w_{\text{probe}} = 0.35$), a shift
of 0.018~hops across a 350-fold change in the weight. For scale, the same oracle moves 0.333~hops between
Throughput and Low-Latency. Probe frugality thus commands roughly 5\% of the leverage of a genuine intent
axis, which is too little to discriminate between paths, and the two profiles are near-duplicates of
Balanced by construction. They remain in the training schedule, where they cost nothing and broaden the
intent space the policy is exposed to, and are excluded from the conditioning studies.

\paragraph{Seed protocol.} \Cref{sec:p2eval:seeds} argues, from four retractions in the next chapter,
that on this class of problem the spread over \emph{training} seeds exceeds the confidence interval
over \emph{evaluation} seeds by an order of magnitude, so a policy must be treated as a draw from a
learner and the learner is the object under test. This chapter applies that rule to itself. Every
agent in the ladder was trained under five random seeds, and \emph{every} quantitative result reported
below --- the conditioning ablation (\cref{tab:p1eval:seeds}), the intent-alignment matrix
(\cref{tab:p1eval:heatmap}), the zero-shot sweep (\cref{fig:p1eval:zeroshot}), the path-count and
order-invariance study (\cref{fig:p1eval:pathcount}), the probing comparison
(\cref{tab:p1eval:probing}), and the congestion ceiling (\cref{fig:p1eval:ceiling}) --- is a mean over
those five seeds with a $t$-based 95\% confidence interval, computed one way throughout. Two
constraints make the seeds comparable. Reseeding touches only torch, numpy, and Python's \texttt{random}:
the environment's pair, hour, and profile streams are held fixed, so every seed sees an identical stream
of training contexts and only the learning process varies. And all five seeds are graded on the same
10752~held-out decision contexts, loaded once and handed to every study of every seed.

Three floats are the exception and are marked where they appear. \Cref{tab:p1eval:ablation} reports the
single reference run, because it carries the full four-intent grid and its two discriminating columns
are re-reported over seeds in \cref{tab:p1eval:seeds} immediately after. \Cref{tab:p1eval:heldout} and
the structurally-held-out zero-shot sweep of \cref{sec:p1eval:zeroshot} are also single-run; they are
generalization checks rather than headline numbers, and \cref{sec:p1eval:limits} states what that
costs them.

Reporting this way changed the chapter. Five claims that held in the reference run do not hold across
five seeds, and we report them as findings (\cref{sec:p1eval:limits} collects them). None is a collapse --- every mechanism the chapter argues for
survives --- but several claims were stated at a precision the seed spread does not support, which is
precisely the failure mode \cref{sec:p2eval:seeds} documents.

\paragraph{Metrics.} For each method we report the intent-weighted reward of \cref{eq:p1reward} and the
chosen path's goodput and latency, goodput in the residual-capacity sense of \cref{sec:p1design:env}.
Intent alignment is assessed two ways: by reward under each
intent, and by \emph{behavioral divergence} -- the degree to which the chosen path changes as the intent
vector changes, holding the decision context fixed. Concretely, writing $d$ for the number of distinct
paths a method selects across the $K = 4$ intents in a given context and $N$ for that context's candidate
count, the divergence is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{div} = \frac{d - 1}{\min(K, N) - 1} \in [0,1],
\label{eq:p1divergence}
\end{equation}
averaged over contexts, so a method that ignores intent scores~0 and one that picks a different path for
every intent scores~1. We also report the \emph{choice entropy}, the Shannon entropy in bits of the
distribution of choices across the four intents, which credits a method for spreading its choices evenly
rather than merely for making more than one. Probing cost is measured by the per-selection probe
accounting of \cref{sec:p1impl:baselines}.

objective; an \emph{unconditioned} path-scoring DQN, which sees no intent at all and isolates what the

Two-Stream-Concat, which routes it to the per-path comparison. \Cref{tab:p1eval:ablation} reports the

\paragraph{Per-path scoring buys the goodput; conditioning buys the re-ranking.} The two contributions
separate cleanly, and the unconditioned scoring agent is what separates them. It reaches
\SI{8.21}{\giga\bit\per\second} of goodput against the flat agent's \SI{5.62}{\giga\bit\per\second},
\emph{with no intent input whatsoever} -- so the large absolute gap between the flat and conditional
agents belongs to the per-path architecture, not to conditioning. Over five seeds the effect is
\SI{8208}{\mega\bit\per\second} [8196, 8220] against \SI{5808}{\mega\bit\per\second} [5711, 5906], an
improvement of 41\% with the intervals far apart. What conditioning adds
is narrower and precisely targeted. On Throughput, Low-Loss, and Balanced the unconditioned scorer is
already statistically indistinguishable from the conditioned one, because those three objectives are all
served by the same high-bandwidth paths. On Low-Latency -- the one evaluated intent that must steer
\emph{away} from the bandwidth-maximizing default -- it falls to 0.679 while Two-Stream-Concat reaches
0.731, cutting mean chosen latency from \SI{17.0}{\milli\second} to \SI{12.7}{\milli\second} at a cost of
roughly \SI{960}{\mega\bit\per\second} of goodput. Its adaptivity of 0.003 confirms it is exactly the
intent-blind agent it should be.

Two qualifications travel with the 41\% wherever it appears: it is residual bottleneck capacity and not
a rate any flow achieved, and it is measured against the control of \cref{sec:p1design:strawman}, so it
bounds what our flat design costs rather than isolating the action space from what that design observes.
The comparison between the unconditioned scorer and the conditional agents is unaffected, since all
three see identical per-path features and differ only in where the intent enters.

\paragraph{The flat architecture is the wrong shape.} The Flat DQN collapses onto a single trade-off: it
scores 0.868 on Low-Loss but only 0.333 on Throughput and 0.561 on Balanced, and its adaptivity of 0.011
confirms it selects essentially the same path whatever intent it is asked to serve. Its reward variance
under Throughput is 0.74, against 0.03 for the unconditioned scorer and 0.003 for the conditioned one --
it is not merely worse on average but wildly inconsistent. As \cref{sec:p1impl:agents} establishes,
this is a property of the design rather than a training artifact: given the identical episode budget on
identical data, the unconditioned scorer reaches sixteen times its final training reward.

How much of that belongs to the action space alone this comparison cannot say. What the claim rests on
instead is what no fixed-action agent can do at any observation: an index into a padded vector cannot be
invariant to the candidates' order, and cannot address a set larger than the head it was compiled with.
\Cref{sec:p1eval:dynamic} shows both consequences directly, so a reader who discounts the 41\% entirely
is still left with them.

None of this is a \emph{model}-capacity effect --- parameters, not bandwidth --- and the direction it runs
in is worth stating because it inverts the trade one would expect. The flat agent carries 85.3\,k parameters; the scoring agents carry
36.2--37.5\,k (\cref{tab:p1eval:ablation}). The architecture that handles a variable path set, serves
every intent from one set of weights, and lifts goodput by 41\% is \emph{less than half the size} of
the fixed-action one it replaces --- because a shared per-path scorer is reused across candidates
where a fixed head must allocate output capacity to each. This is the one comparison the control's
weakness does not threaten, and in fact understates: the flat agent reaches 85.3\,k parameters from a
five-dimensional input, so giving it the per-path observation it lacks would enlarge it further while
the scoring agents already consume seven features per candidate at 37.5\,k. The dynamic-input design is
therefore not a flexibility that is paid for in model size; on this problem it is cheaper on both axes at
once, which is the rare case where the deployability argument and the performance argument point the same
way.

\paragraph{Where the intent enters decides whether it acts.} Both conditional agents receive the same
intent vector and both recover strong reward on every intent. The decisive difference is how much they
\emph{act} on it: Two-Stream-Concat attains an adaptivity of 0.149 and a choice entropy of 0.375~bits
against Value-Concat's 0.050 and 0.130 -- a factor of three in re-ranking. This is the predicted
signature of \cref{prop:p1cancel}: an intent entering only the value stream shifts every path's score by
the same amount and cancels under the $\arg\max$, so Value-Concat can influence its own choice only
through the indirect effect of training and through the single trust feature that leaks $w_3, w_4$ into
$f_i$. The behavioral consequence shows up as reward exactly where re-ranking is required -- on
Low-Latency, 0.731 against 0.708.

\paragraph{How much re-ranking is available?} Adaptivity lives on $[0,1]$, so 0.149 looks small in
isolation. The oracle sets the scale: a policy with perfect knowledge, re-optimizing per context under
each intent, attains an adaptivity of only 0.161 in this environment, because most contexts have no
better answer to give a different intent. Taking both quantities over five seeds
(\cref{tab:p1eval:seeds}), Two-Stream-Concat captures \textbf{87\%} of the achievable re-ranking
(0.1398 against the oracle's 0.1609) and Value-Concat \textbf{26\%}. The reference run puts these at
93\% and 31\%; as elsewhere in this chapter, it sits at the favorable end of the seed distribution, and
the seeded figures are the ones to quote.

\paragraph{Distance from optimal, and how much room there was.} Reading \cref{tab:p1eval:ablation}
against its oracle row, Two-Stream-Concat's shortfall is \textbf{0.0002} on Throughput, \textbf{0.0018}
on Low-Latency, \textbf{0.0012} on Low-Loss, and \textbf{0.0005} on Balanced. These are the reference
run's; on the two intents \cref{tab:p1eval:seeds} re-reports over five seeds the shortfall is 0.0022 on
Throughput and 0.0034 on Low-Latency, so the seeded figures are the ones to quote and they are roughly
twice the single run's. We give these as absolute
reward differences rather than as a percentage of the oracle, because \cref{eq:p1reward} is shifted into
$[-1,1]$ and then clamped: its zero is an artifact of the shift, so a ratio taken against it denotes
nothing physical, however small the ratio looks.

The shortfalls are small, and the honest reading is that they are small \emph{on a task with little
headroom} --- which is the claim the data support, and a stronger one than near-optimality asserted
without a scale. The oracle sets that scale twice over. Its own adaptivity is 0.161
(\cref{tab:p1eval:ablation}), so in the large majority of contexts a policy with perfect foreknowledge
has no better answer to give a different intent, and three of the four evaluated intents are served by
the same high-bandwidth paths anyway (\cref{sec:p1eval:intent}). What the numbers establish is therefore
that the conditioned selector leaves almost nothing on the table \emph{where there is anything to leave},
while inspecting one path rather than all thirty --- not that the problem was hard and it solved it.

Two findings matter here. First, the scoring agents generalize structurally: rewards fall on Low-Latency
(0.731 to 0.607), but the \emph{oracle} falls with them (0.733 to 0.610), and Two-Stream-Concat's
optimality gap grows only from 0.0018 to 0.0033. The absolute drop is a property of what these pairs make
available, not of the policy. Adaptivity in fact rises, from 0.149 to 0.190, tracking the oracle's own
rise to 0.181. Second, the flat agent does not generalize at all: its Throughput reward goes
\emph{negative}, from 0.333 to $-0.085$. We attribute this to the observation rather than to the action
space, which is the reverse of what the fixed-action framing would suggest. Our control never receives
the source--destination pair (\cref{sec:p1design:strawman}), so on pairs it has not memorized there is
nothing in its input to distinguish one candidate index from another, and an index-addressed policy with
no pair identity has no mechanism for transferring what it learned. The finding is therefore evidence
about \emph{this} control and not about fixed action spaces in general; what it does show is how little
survives when a policy addresses paths by position while observing only aggregates, which is the
combination \cref{sec:p1design:strawman} argues against.

column spanning 0.011 in total cannot resolve a 0.0011 margin, which the single run had no way to reveal.
The figure marks both non-significant diagonals so it cannot be read as showing four wins.

\paragraph{Why the loss axis is degenerate, and why better profiles would not fix it.} The reason
Low-Loss discriminates so weakly is not a poor choice of profile but the environment: of the
43008~selections each seed makes, \textbf{98.44\%} land on a path with exactly zero loss, averaged over
the five seeds, so there is almost nothing for a loss-averse intent to avoid. Consistently, the latency-optimal and loss-optimal paths coincide in 82.8\%
of contexts, whereas a genuine bandwidth/latency conflict exists in \textbf{59\%}. It is tempting to
propose replacing Low-Loss with a profile whose optimum conflicts with high bandwidth -- a hop-count-averse
or trust-dominant intent -- but the reward function of \cref{eq:p1reward} exposes only three levers, and
hop count enters solely through the probe charge. We tested two such candidates against the
\emph{oracle}, which bounds what any policy could achieve: a \texttt{probe\_extreme} profile at sixteen
times the \texttt{probe\_averse} weight moves the oracle's chosen hop count by 0.009~hops, and a pure
loss-aversion profile with the latency term removed collapses onto the throughput optimum, matching it to
within \SI{2}{\mega\bit\per\second} of goodput. Since the oracle bounds every policy, no reweighting of
this reward can produce a fourth discriminating intent. The honest statement is therefore that
\emph{this environment offers one genuine behavioral trade-off axis, goodput against latency}, that
intent alignment is demonstrated on it, and that making loss or hop count discriminating requires
changing the reward or the environment -- adding an explicit hop-count term, or raising offered load until
loss is common, given that only 7.8\% of links are oversubscribed today. We return to this in
\cref{ch:conclusion}.

every seed and its magnitude varies almost fourfold, which is worth knowing before treating any single
number here as the effect size. Buying \SI{4.9}{\milli\second} of latency means accepting shorter, more
heavily loaded paths. The mechanism works; it is the loss axis that has nothing to give.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Generalization to Unseen Intents}
\label{sec:p1eval:zeroshot}

Every intent evaluated so far is one of the six profiles the agent trained on. That establishes only that
a single network can store six behaviors -- not that it has learned a \emph{mapping} from the intent
space to behavior. This section tests the stronger property that \cref{sec:problem:properties} requires:
presented with a weight vector it has never seen, does the policy behave in the correspondingly
interpolated way, without retraining?

We sweep the intent along the segment between the two profiles that genuinely conflict,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}(t) = (1-t)\cdot\mathbf{w}_{\text{Throughput}} + t\cdot\mathbf{w}_{\text{Low-Latency}},
\qquad t \in \{-0.2,\, -0.1,\, 0.0,\, 0.1,\, \dots,\, 1.1,\, 1.2\},
\label{eq:p1interp}
\end{equation}
and evaluate each of the 15~resulting weight vectors on the same 10752~held-out contexts. Only $t = 0$
and $t = 1$ are vectors any agent has ever seen; the eleven interior points are unseen interpolations and
the two outside $[0,1]$ are extrapolations beyond both trained profiles.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{p1eval_zeroshot.png}
  \caption[Behavior along the unseen intent segment]{Behavior of the conditioned policies along the unseen intent segment of \cref{eq:p1interp}.
  Thin lines are the five individual training seeds, the heavy line their mean, and the band the 95\%
  confidence interval over seeds; only the two dashed verticals were trained on, the shaded region is
  interpolation and the flanks are extrapolation. Chosen latency falls smoothly and, in four of five
  seeds, strictly monotonically --- the signature of a learned mapping rather than of memorized
  endpoints. Per-seed traces are drawn so that monotonicity can be checked run by run rather than only in
  the aggregate. Value-Concat interpolates smoothly too, but traverses roughly a third as much behavior,
  as \cref{prop:p1cancel} predicts.}
  \label{fig:p1eval:zeroshot}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:p1eval:zeroshot} shows the result. The conditioned policy's mean chosen latency falls from
\SI{17.79}{\milli\second} [17.26, 18.32] at $t = 0$ to \SI{12.89}{\milli\second} [12.33, 13.44] at
$t = 1$, a span of \SI{4.90}{\milli\second} [4.16, 5.64]. The descent is strictly monotone in four of the
five seeds ($\rho = -1.000$); the fifth has a single ascending step, between $t = 0.0$ and $t = 0.1$, of
\SI{0.036}{\milli\second} --- 0.6\% of that seed's span --- giving $\rho = -0.991$. The reference run
this chapter was drafted from was one of the four, and reporting $\rho = -1.000$ as an exact property of
the policy would therefore have been an artifact of which seed was read.

Monotonicity alone is a weak test, so we also ask how the change is distributed. No seed concentrates
more than 31.0\% of its span in a single step of the sweep (mean 24.2\% [19.3, 29.2]). A policy that had
memorized six points would instead sit at one endpoint behavior until the weight vector crossed some
threshold and then jump, concentrating essentially the whole span in one step; the worst of five seeds
puts under a third of it there. This is the claim the test was built to make, and it survives --- but at
31\% rather than at the reference run's 24.7\%, which was near the favorable end of the seed
distribution. The endpoints reproduce the trained-profile behavior, matching the Throughput and
Low-Latency rows of \cref{sec:p1eval:intent}, and the extrapolated points continue the trend rather than
diverging -- \SI{11.98}{\milli\second} [11.43, 12.53] at $t = 1.2$, beyond any trained intent.

Two further observations. First, the same sweep on the 32~structurally held-out pairs of
\cref{sec:p1eval:setup} reproduces the result: \SI{24.76}{\milli\second} to \SI{19.16}{\milli\second},
again monotone with $\rho = -1.000$. This is \emph{unseen intents on unseen pairs simultaneously}, and it
is the strongest generalization evidence in the chapter --- with the caveat that it is the one zero-shot
number still read from the single reference run, so the exact $\rho$ should be read as that run's, not as
a property of the learner. Second, the Value-Concat control interpolates smoothly as well, and in all
five seeds strictly monotonically, but over a span of only \SI{1.56}{\milli\second} [1.22, 1.91] against
Two-Stream-Concat's \SI{4.90}{\milli\second} --- a factor of \textbf{3.13}. This is \cref{prop:p1cancel}
showing up from a third direction: a policy whose conditioning cannot re-rank directly can still shift
behavior through the indirect effects of training, but it covers roughly a third as much of the behavior
space.

We therefore claim zero-shot generalization in a specific and bounded sense: interpolation, and modest
extrapolation, along the segment between two trained profiles, on one topology. We have not tested
weight vectors far outside the trained convex hull, nor a second topology.

\subsection{Variable Path Sets}
\label{sec:p1eval:dynamic}

The scoring architecture of \cref{sec:p1design:scoring} was adopted so that one policy could serve pairs
with any number of candidate paths, in any order, without padding or retraining. So far that claim rests
on the architecture's construction. This section measures it.

Binning the held-out decisions by their natural candidate count is uninformative here, because 7988 of
the 8010~routable pairs have exactly 30~paths -- the topology simply does not vary $N$. We therefore
restrict the candidate set \emph{at decision time} to $N \in \{2, 4, 8, 16, 30\}$, presenting every
method the identical subsets, and measure each method's regret against the best path available within
that restricted set. This is a harder test than natural variation would give, since the agents trained
exclusively on 30-candidate sets and every smaller value of $N$ is off-distribution.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{p1eval_pathcount.png}
  \caption[Regret against candidate-set size]{Mean regret against the best path in the presented candidate set, by set size (log scale,
  10752~decisions per point, Balanced intent). Heavy lines are the mean over five training seeds and
  faint markers the individual seeds. One model per method, no retraining and no padding, across
  a 15-fold range in $N$. Every scoring agent stays two orders of magnitude below the flat DQN, which must
  address a fixed 30-way action head and degrades as $N$ moves into the middle of its range; the
  separation is disjoint in confidence intervals at every $N$.}
  \label{fig:p1eval:pathcount}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:p1eval:pathcount} reports the result. Every scoring agent holds mean regret between 0.0003 and
0.011 across the whole range, while the flat DQN sits between 0.239 [0.226, 0.251] and 0.516 [0.480,
0.552] -- two orders of magnitude worse, and worst in the middle of its range, where the padded action
head is least well matched to the number of real alternatives. The gap is disjoint in confidence
intervals at every $N$. One model, no retraining, no padding, across a 15-fold change in the action
count.

One caveat: the unconditioned scorer and Two-Stream-Concat
are slightly \emph{better} at $N = 30$ (0.0003 and 0.0016) than at small $N$ (0.005--0.011). They trained
only on 30-candidate sets, and the per-path bandwidth-ratio feature is normalized by the maximum within
the presented set, so subsampling shifts the observation distribution off what they saw. The effect is
real but bounded at about 0.01~reward: the architecture handles unseen $N$ gracefully rather than
perfectly. Value-Concat is the exception --- its regret is flat across $N$ (0.005--0.009) with
overlapping intervals throughout --- so the off-distribution penalty is a property of the better-adapted
policies rather than of the scoring architecture as such.

\paragraph{Order invariance.} The second half of the property is that the choice must not depend on the
order in which candidates happen to be enumerated. We re-scored each of the 10752~contexts under three
random permutations of the path ordering --- 32256~trials per agent per seed, 161280~per agent over the
five seeds, \textbf{483840}~in total --- and checked whether each agent selected the same \emph{underlying
path}. All three scoring and conditional agents did so in \textbf{100.0000\%} of trials, with zero
mismatches in any seed: exact invariance, not approximate. This is the one exactness claim in the chapter
that survives being stated as exact, and it should be, because it follows from the architecture's
permutation equivariance rather than from anything training produced. The flat DQN is excluded by
construction: its action is an index into a padded vector, so permuting the candidates changes what it
selects, which is precisely the architectural point.

reward are directly comparable, then under all four.

Throughput, 0.0098 on Low-Loss, and 0.0050 on Balanced --- and then falls 0.0806 behind on Low-Latency,
because maximizing bandwidth is simply the wrong rule when the objective is delay. Lowest-latency is the
mirror image: it wins Low-Latency outright and then trails by 0.535 on Throughput and 0.267 on Balanced.
So the four columns are covered by two heuristics with opposite failure modes, not by four specialists,
and no single one of them is usable across the set. Selecting
the right heuristic per intent would require knowing the intent \emph{and} a switching mechanism; the
learned selector absorbs both into one policy, at one probe instead of thirty and at a lower absolute
measurement cost than any of them. For the conditioned selector we therefore recover, and extend across
intents, the high-quality-at-low-probing property of Bourak~et~al.~\cite{Bourak2025}.

\subsection{What Five Seeds Changed}
\label{sec:p1eval:limits}

This chapter was first drafted from a single reference training run and then re-measured across five,
following the rule \cref{sec:p2eval:seeds} derives from the next chapter's retractions. Five sharp claims
did not survive, and we collect them here rather than leaving a reader to reconstruct which numbers moved.
They divide into two kinds, and the distinction is the useful part.

\paragraph{Claims stated as exact that are not.} The intent-alignment diagonal wins two of four columns
across seeds rather than all four (\cref{sec:p1eval:intent}); the zero-shot Spearman correlation is
$-1.000$ in four seeds and $-0.991$ in the fifth (\cref{sec:p1eval:zeroshot}); and no seed keeps its
largest interpolation step under 24.7\%, the worst reaching 31.0\%. In each case the phenomenon survives
with room to spare and only the exactness fails. The pattern is not accidental: all three were read off a
quantity whose seed spread the single run gave no way to see, and two of the three sit on the
low-discrimination objectives the chapter had already flagged as weak.

\paragraph{Claims read off the best seed.} The learned selector beats every heuristic on two of four
intents in every seed rather than three (\cref{sec:p1eval:overhead}); its Low-Latency shortfall behind
lowest-latency is 0.0031 [0.0015, 0.0048] rather than 0.0015; and its goodput sits 0.74\% [0.48, 1.00]
behind widest-path rather than within 0.5\%. On each, the reference run is at or near the favorable end
of the five-seed distribution. Nothing was cherry-picked --- these are what one gets by training once,
reading the number, and writing it down, which is exactly the mechanism \cref{sec:p2eval:seeds}
documents. Measuring it in both of the thesis's systems is what makes it a finding about the method
rather than an anecdote about one of them.

\paragraph{What did not move.} The chapter's four summary claims are untouched. The architectural gap
between flat and per-path scoring holds with disjoint intervals on Throughput and in regret at every path
count. \Cref{prop:p1cancel}'s re-ranking prediction holds with disjoint intervals on adaptivity and on
Low-Latency reward. Zero-shot interpolation holds as a qualitative property, and order invariance holds
as an exact one across all 483840~permutation trials. The congestion ceiling of \cref{sec:p1eval:ceiling}
reproduces to within a confidence interval 0.6~percentage points wide. What five seeds cost this chapter
is the third decimal place, and what they buy is that the remaining decimals can be defended.

One ordering deserves a note here, because it is stable across seeds and a reader scanning
\cref{tab:p1eval:seeds} will find it: the flat--scoring gap is not a gap on \emph{every} intent. On
Low-Latency the flat control's 0.7026 [0.6918, 0.7134] sits \emph{above} the unconditioned scorer's
0.6798 [0.6755, 0.6841]. This is not a point in the control's favor. A policy that has collapsed onto one
low-bandwidth trade-off (\cref{sec:p1eval:ablation}) happens to land near the latency-averse answer, and
scores there for the same reason it scores 0.373 on Throughput.

\section{The Single-Path Performance Ceiling}
\label{sec:p1eval:ceiling}

The results so far show that one conditioned policy can serve the evaluated intents at low probing
cost. This final experiment asks the question that motivates the next chapter: how far does the best
single-path choice carry an application as the network fills up? We bin every held-out decision into six
equal-count bins by its realized congestion -- the mean utilization across the flow's candidate paths at
that hour -- and plot the goodput each method achieves against it (\cref{fig:p1eval:ceiling}). Each bin
holds 1792~decisions per method. Because the environment allows links to be oversubscribed, capping
utilization at 1.5 rather than 1.0 (\cref{sec:p1design:env}), the upper three bins sit above 1.0: they are
hours in which the aggregate demand offered to a flow's candidate paths exceeds their capacity.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{p1eval_ceiling.png}
  \caption[Goodput and reward against realized congestion]{Goodput (left) and intent-weighted reward (right) against realized congestion,
  1792~decisions per bin per method, mean over five training seeds with 95\% confidence bands (narrower
  than the line width for every method). As throughout the chapter, the left axis is residual bottleneck
  capacity on the chosen path (\cref{sec:p1design:env}) and not a rate a flow delivered --- the
  distinction the paragraph after this figure returns to. The learned selector tracks the widest-path ceiling at every
  load, but that ceiling itself falls by about 25\% from light to heavy congestion: past a point, no
  single path can carry the demand. The right panel is the bridging argument of this thesis in one picture --
  the selector's \emph{reward} stays flat while its goodput descends, because the reward measures
  selection quality and is blind to the ceiling by construction. The policy is doing its job; the action
  space is the limit.}
  \label{fig:p1eval:ceiling}
\end{figure}

Two effects appear in the goodput panel. First, the learned selector tracks the widest-path ceiling
across the entire congestion range, confirming that it makes near-optimal use of whatever single-path
capacity exists, while the load-blind heuristics fall away sharply -- shortest-path, lowest-latency, and
the SCION default lose roughly 55--58\% of their goodput from the lightest to the heaviest congestion bin,
ECMP degrades more gracefully but still loses about 39\%, and random collapses entirely. Second, and
crucially, \emph{the ceiling itself descends}: both the learned selector and widest-path drop from about
\SI{9.1}{\giga\bit\per\second} at light load to about \SI{6.9}{\giga\bit\per\second} at heavy load. For
the learned selector that is \SI{9100}{\mega\bit\per\second} [9068, 9133] to \SI{6862}{\mega\bit\per\second}
[6847, 6878], a loss of \textbf{24.6\%} [24.3, 24.9] over five training seeds; widest-path loses 25.3\%.
This is the most robust result in the chapter, and unsurprisingly so, since the ceiling is a property of
the environment that the policy tracks rather than a property of the policy. This is the single-path
performance ceiling. When the shared bottleneck is congested, the
best available path is still one path, and one path can carry only so much; no amount of intent-aware
selection recovers capacity that a single path cannot provide. Escaping this ceiling requires spreading a
flow across several paths at once -- the problem \cref{ch:multipath} takes up.

\paragraph{What this bridge is, and what it is not.} Because the whole thesis turns on this transition,
three limits belong with it. First, the ceiling is measured in a model that cannot represent the remedy: the link model is
open-loop (\cref{sec:p1design:env}), so a multipath flow's own contention across its subflows is not
representable, and nothing in this environment demonstrates that splitting recovers any of the lost
24.6\%. Second, \cref{ch:multipath} answers the question on a different data plane, with a different
topology, different traffic, and a different reward, so no quantity crosses the chapter boundary: the
bridge is conceptual rather than empirical, and the two chapters share an architecture and an idea, not a
number. Third, the descending quantity is \emph{residual bottleneck capacity} available to a flow that
places no load of its own --- not a delivered rate that any application achieved.

What survives all three is the claim the chapter actually needs, and it is the one the right panel of
\cref{fig:p1eval:ceiling} makes on its own: the binding constraint here is the \emph{action space}, not
the policy. The selector's reward stays flat while the capacity it can reach falls by a quarter, so no
better single-path policy and no better reward recovers what is lost --- only a larger action. The
experiment that would convert this argument into a result is worth naming even though we do not run it:
replay these same heavy-congestion contexts allowing the flow to be split across the top-$k$ candidates
under the same link model, and report the aggregate residual capacity available to a split against a
single choice. It needs no learning and no new environment, only the existing path store and link
states, and it would let this chapter close its own argument instead of deferring it.

\paragraph{Why the reward does not see the ceiling.} The right panel of \cref{fig:p1eval:ceiling} tells
the complementary story, and a reader who takes the reward as an absolute performance measure will find
the two panels contradictory. The learned selector's reward is
essentially flat across the congestion range, 0.8976 [0.8958, 0.8993] to 0.8883 [0.8870, 0.8896], while
its goodput falls by a quarter --- a reward drop of 0.0093 against a goodput drop of 24.6\%. This
is a direct consequence of \cref{eq:p1reward}, in which goodput is normalized against the best candidate
path at the same hour (\cref{sec:p1design:mdp}): a policy that keeps choosing the best available path
scores near the top whatever that path can carry. The heuristics, which do not keep choosing the best
available path, collapse on reward as well -- shortest-path from 0.72 to 0.31, and the fixed-random
baseline from 0.26 to $-0.32$. The ceiling is therefore invisible to the objective the agent optimizes,
which is precisely why breaking it takes a change of \emph{action space} rather than a better policy or a
better reward.

\paragraph{Summary.} This chapter established four claims, each measured over five training seeds. First,
a fixed-objective, fixed-action DQN is the wrong shape for this problem. A permutation-equivariant
per-path scorer gives exactly invariant selection under all 483840~permutation trials and holds regret
between 0.0003 and 0.011 from $N = 2$ to $N = 30$ against the fixed-action control's 0.239--0.516 --- two
properties a padded action head cannot have at any observation --- and it raises residual bottleneck
capacity by
41\%, from \SI{5.81}{\giga\bit\per\second} to \SI{8.21}{\giga\bit\per\second} with disjoint confidence
intervals, against a control that is also pair-blind and sees no per-path features
(\cref{sec:p1design:strawman}), so that last figure bounds our flat design rather than the action space
alone. Second, making one policy serve many intents is not a matter of feeding it the intent:
by \cref{prop:p1cancel}, conditioning that enters only path-independent terms cancels under the
$\arg\max$, and the value-stream variant re-ranks roughly three times less than one whose conditioning
reaches the per-path comparison, with disjoint confidence intervals, and traverses roughly a third as much
of the behavior space along the intent segment. Third, the conditioned policy generalizes over the intent
\emph{space} rather than merely across trained points: behavior interpolates smoothly and, in four of
five seeds, strictly monotonically between trained intents, with no seed concentrating more than 31\% of
the span in one step, and it does so on pairs never seen in training. Fourth, it does this at one probe
per decision instead of thirty --- a $39.6\times$ cut in measurement cost for 0.74\% of goodput --- while
beating every heuristic on two of four intents in every seed and on three of four on the mean, and
trailing the purpose-built heuristic on the fourth by 0.003.

Three scoping caveats belong with these claims. This environment offers exactly one genuine behavioral
trade-off axis -- goodput against latency -- so intent alignment is demonstrated there rather than across
all four evaluated profiles, and \cref{sec:p1eval:intent} shows why no reweighting of this reward could
change that; over five seeds the alignment diagonal is significant on exactly those two objectives that
demand re-ranking and on neither of the other two. Every result rests on a single topology. And the
precision of several claims fell when the chapter moved from one training run to five
(\cref{sec:p1eval:limits}), which is a finding about how results on this class of problem should be
reported as much as about this system. What survives all three caveats is the chapter's
final finding: even a near-optimal single-path selector loses about a quarter of its goodput under heavy
congestion -- 24.6\% [24.3, 24.9], the tightest interval in the chapter -- because a single path is a
hard capacity bound: the ceiling that \cref{ch:multipath} sets out to break.
